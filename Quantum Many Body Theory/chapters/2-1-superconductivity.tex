\chapter{BCS Theory of Superconductivity}

Superconductivity was experimentally observed well before the formal discovery of quantum mechanics, presenting a profound physical mystery that eventually necessitated the development of a comprehensive quantum many-body description. The definitive microscopic explanation of this phenomenon, known as the BCS theory, was developed in 1957 by John Bardeen, Leon Cooper, and John Robert Schrieffer. At its core, the theory explains the superconducting state through the formation of bound states of electrons, known as Cooper pairs.

To formally develop this, we will consider a general system composed of spin-1/2 fermions experiencing a mutual \emph{attractive} interaction between particles of opposite spin. Our primary theoretical interest is to determine the low-temperature phase of this many-body system. The central idea is that this mutual attraction can induce the formation of Cooper pairs—pairs of fermions with opposite spin. Because these composite pairs possess an integer total spin, they behave effectively as bosons and can undergo macroscopic condensation at sufficiently low temperatures.

The original motivation for studying such an interacting fermionic system was the microscopic explanation of metallic superconductivity. Superconductivity is a physical phenomenon occurring in certain metals and compounds whereby an electric current flows with absolutely zero electrical resistance below a specific critical temperature \( T_c \). This phenomenon is deeply analogous to superfluidity; whereas a superfluid involves the macroscopic flow of a fluid with zero viscosity (the mechanical analog of electrical resistance) below a critical temperature, a superconductor involves the frictionless flow of charged carriers, namely electrons.

A crucial thermodynamic clue pointing toward the existence of these bound pairs of fermions in a superconductor is provided by the electronic specific heat, \( c_v \). For an ideal Fermi gas at low temperatures \( T \)—which serves as a highly effective model for the conduction electron gas in a normal solid—the specific heat scales linearly with \( T \). However, when a material transitions into a superconductor, the specific heat is observed to vanish exponentially with decreasing temperature. This exponential suppression is the hallmark thermodynamic behavior expected when there is a finite energy gap in the excitation spectrum separating the macroscopic ground state from the first available excited states. The magnitude of this energy gap can be extracted directly from specific heat measurements, revealing it to be extremely small—typically several orders of magnitude smaller—compared to the Fermi energy \( E_F \) of the electrons. It is natural to guess that this energy gap is directly associated with the finite binding energy of the fermion pairs. (The microscopic mechanism by which normally repulsive electrons can experience an effective mutual attraction in a metal lattice will be discussed in detail in the subsequent section).

Beyond its foundational success in condensed matter systems, the BCS theoretical framework we are going to develop is remarkably universal. The collection of phenomena that can be governed by these same equations spans an astonishing range of more than ten orders of magnitude in temperature. At the lower extreme, in the nanokelvin regime, the theory applies directly to ultracold Fermi gases trapped in optical lattices. These are dilute, neutral systems of fermionic atoms where an effective mutual attraction—arising fundamentally from van der Waals forces—can be accurately modeled as a simple contact interaction due to the extreme diluteness of the gas. These highly tunable systems have been studied very actively over recent years because experimentalists can artificially tune the strength of the interparticle interaction at will. The theory is equally relevant to other macroscopic neutral systems, such as liquid \( ^3\text{He} \), where fermionic neutral atoms also experience a mutual attraction. At the absolute opposite extreme of the temperature and density scale, the very same BCS formalism can be extended to describe the pairing of quarks inside the ultra-dense, extremely high-temperature cores of neutron stars (a phenomenon known as color superconductivity).

\section{Basic Model for Effective Attraction}

Let us consider a simplified model of a solid consisting of a cubic lattice of positively charged ions with a lattice spacing \( a \), permeated by a gas of itinerant electrons. We assume the system is isolated, without any external electric or magnetic fields. The electrons are subject to a periodic potential generated by the ionic lattice; consequently, the potential minima are located in the immediate vicinity of the positive ions, marking the regions where the electrons are most likely to be found.

The electrons interact not only with this periodic ionic potential but also with one another via the standard Coulomb repulsion, \( V_{e-e} \sim e^2/r \). However, the presence of the mobile electron gas embedded in the positively charged background activates the many-body phenomenon of screening. In a metal, the spatial redistribution of conduction electrons around any single negative charge effectively screens that negative charge at a distance from it. This screening effect drastically modifies the long-range Coulomb repulsion into a short-range effective interaction, which decays exponentially as \( \sim e^{-r/\xi} \), where the screening length \( \xi \) is typically on the order of a few lattice spacings \( a \). Although this screened interaction is significantly weaker at macroscopic distances, it remains fundamentally repulsive. To explain the formation of Cooper pairs, we must identify a physical mechanism capable of overcoming this residual repulsion to produce a net effective attraction between the electrons.

\subsubsection{The Electron-Phonon Interaction and Fröhlich Mechanism}

This requisite attraction arises from the interaction between the electrons and the collective vibrations of the ionic lattice, known as phonons. Because the typical Debye temperature characterizing these macroscopic phonon modes is on the order of \( \omega_D \sim 100\,\text{K} \), while superconducting phenomena typically occur at cryogenic temperatures around \( 1\,\text{K} \), the thermal phonons are effectively frozen out. Consequently, we can safely treat the quantized phonon field as a perturbation to the interacting electron system.

As famously demonstrated by H. Fröhlich, in certain metals, the combination of the screening of the Coulomb repulsion and the virtual exchange of phonons between electrons may lead to an overall effective attraction between them. Specifically, the dynamic interaction between electrons and phonons produces a retarded attractive force. When an electron moves through the crystal, it locally modifies the ionic lattice by pulling the positive ions slightly toward its trajectory, creating a localized cloud of positive charges, and then rapidly moves away. Subsequently, a second electron may be electrostatically attracted by this localized accumulation of positive charge.

A key physical ingredient of this retarded mechanism is the stark difference in dynamical time scales: the heavy distortions of the ionic lattice fade away rather slowly in comparison with the extremely fast time scale over which one electron is replaced by a second one at a certain position due to the rapid motion of the electrons. Ultimately, these two combined effects—electronic screening and virtual phonon exchange—may lead in certain materials to an overall effective attraction between the electrons, provided those electrons belong to a tiny energy shell situated immediately around the Fermi energy \( E_F \).


\subsection{Effective Interaction}

We aim to describe the behavior of electrons in a solid and understand how they interact with the lattice vibrations. A solid can be modeled as a static lattice of ions, with electrons occupying localized orbitals and tunneling through them. The classical positions of the ions are perturbed by dynamical excitations, which we describe as \textbf{phonons}.

In the harmonic approximation, the phonon Hamiltonian is constructed as a sum over independent harmonic oscillators:
\[
    H_{\text{ion}} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\mathbf{q}} \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}} a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger a_{\mathbf{q}},
\]
where \(a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger\) and \(a_{\mathbf{q}}\) are the bosonic creation and annihilation operators for a phonon with momentum \(\mathbf{q}\), and \(\omega_{\mathbf{q}}\) is the phonon frequency.

The Hamiltonian for the electrons is composed of an interaction part and a kinetic part:
\[
    H_{\text{el}} = H_{\text{int}} + H_{\text{kin}}.
\]

\paragraph{Interaction Hamiltonian.} Naively, the interaction between electrons is the bare Coulomb repulsion. However, the presence of a background of positive charges screens this interaction. The effective screened potential is often described by the Thomas-Fermi model, which introduces an exponential decay over the distance \(|\mathbf{r}|\):
\[
    V(\mathbf{r}) \propto \frac{e^{-|\mathbf{r}|/\xi}}{|\mathbf{r}|} \sim e^{-|\mathbf{r}|/\xi},
\]
where \(\xi\) is the Thomas-Fermi screening length.

While the kinetic energy can be formulated on a real-space lattice (yielding the Hubbard model), it is much more convenient to diagonalize it directly in momentum space. Assuming that the kinetic energy is independent of the electron spin \(\sigma\), we have:
\[
    H_{\text{kin}} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma},
\]
where \(c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}^\dagger\) and \(c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}\) are the fermionic creation and annihilation operators for an electron with momentum \(\mathbf{k}\) and spin \(\sigma\), and \(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m}\) is the bare electron dispersion.

\paragraph{Total Hamiltonian.} Combining the lattice and electronic components, the total Hamiltonian is:
\[
    H = H_{\text{ion}} + H_{\text{int}} + H_{\text{kin}}.
\]
Our ultimate goal is to use perturbation theory to trace out the phonon degrees of freedom. This procedure will yield an effective Hamiltonian that only contains the fermionic operators \((c, c^\dagger)\), effectively incorporating the phonons as a mediated interaction between the electrons:
\[
    H \to H_{\text{eff}}(c, c^\dagger).
\]

\subsection{Single Particle Problem}

\paragraph{Electron-Phonon Interaction.} How can an electron interact with a phonon? By traveling through the solid, an electron can kick the ions and excite their physical modes. Based on the principle of Occam's razor, we consider only the simplest fundamental processes: the emission and the absorption of a single phonon.

The phenomenological interaction potential \(V_1\) describing these scattering processes must contain a sum over all possible electron momenta \(\mathbf{k}\), spins \(\sigma\), and phonon momenta \(\mathbf{q}\):
\[
    V_1 = \sum_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} \sum_{\mathbf{q}} \left( M_{\mathbf{q}, \sigma} a_{\mathbf{q}} c_{\mathbf{k} + \mathbf{q}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} + M_{-\mathbf{q}, \sigma} a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{q}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} \right).
\]
The amplitude of this interaction is governed by the matrix element \(M_{\mathbf{q}, \sigma}\), which takes the form:
\[
    M_{\mathbf{q}, \sigma} \simeq \frac{|\mathbf{q}|}{\sqrt{M_{\text{ion}} \omega_{\mathbf{q}}}}.
\]
Physically, \(M_{\mathbf{q}, \sigma}\) is inversely proportional to the square root of the ion mass \(M_{\text{ion}}\). If the lattice is composed of heavy ions, it is extremely challenging for the electrons to excite the phononic modes, causing the matrix element to vanish rapidly. Conversely, lighter ions allow for an easier transfer of energy from the electrons to the ion chain.

\paragraph{Perturbation Theory in $V_1$.} Our full system is described by:
\[
    H = H_{\text{kin}} + H_{\text{ph}} + V_1.
\]
We proceed by treating \(V_1\) as a perturbation. To do so, we must first define the unperturbed vacuum (ground state) for both fermions and bosons.

\paragraph{Unperturbed Vacuum.} We evaluate the vacuum in the presence of some initial particle excitations. This state, \(|\phi_{n_e, n_{ph}}\rangle\), is formed by the tensor product of the electron and phonon states:
\[
    |\phi_{n_e, n_{ph}}\rangle = \left( \prod_{\substack{\mathbf{k}_j = \mathbf{k}_1, \dots, \mathbf{k}_{n_e} \\ \sigma_j = \sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_{n_e}}} c_{\mathbf{k}_j, \sigma_j}^\dagger \right) \left( \prod_{\mathbf{q}_j = \mathbf{q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{q}_{n_{ph}}} a_{\mathbf{q}_j}^\dagger \right) |0, 0\rangle.
\]
Here, the absolute vacuum is defined as \(|0, 0\rangle = |n_e = 0, n_{ph} = 0\rangle\). The phonon vacuum itself is a tensor product over all possible momentum states:
\[
    |0, 0\rangle = |0\rangle_{\mathbf{q}_1} \otimes |0\rangle_{\mathbf{q}_2} \otimes \dots
\]
Because the system is confined in a box/lattice, the allowed momenta are discrete and defined within the Brillouin zone. For example, along the \(x\)-direction:
\[
    q_x^{(J)} = \frac{2\pi J}{L_x}, \quad J \in \mathbb{Z}.
\]
For the electrons, this highly populated unperturbed ground state is commonly referred to as the \textbf{Fermi Sea}.

\subsubsection{First Order Perturbation Theory}

Let us compute the first-order correction to the energy, which fundamentally requires evaluating the expectation value of the perturbation \(V_1\) over the unperturbed vacuum state \(|\phi_0\rangle\):
\[
    E_1 = \langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle.
\]
If we define our initial state explicitly by its definite number of electrons and phonons, \(|\phi_0\rangle = |n_e, n_{ph}\rangle\), applying the interaction potential \(V_1\) physically yields a new state where the total number of phonons has changed by exactly one (due to the fundamental processes of either a single emission or a single absorption):
\[
    V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle = |n_e, n_{ph} \pm 1\rangle.
\]
Because the number of phonons strictly changes under the action of \(V_1\), the resulting state is mathematically orthogonal to the initial state. Under the physically motivated assumption that the phonons do not macroscopically condense (which would give them a finite vacuum expectation value), the first-order energy correction strictly vanishes:
\[
    \implies \langle n_e, n_{ph} | n_e, n_{ph} \pm 1 \rangle = 0.
\]

\subsubsection{Second Order Perturbation Theory}

Since the leading first-order term is identically zero, we must evaluate the perturbation to second order to capture the leading interaction effects. The second-order energy correction \(E_2\) is formally given by summing over all possible intermediate states \(|\phi_{n,m}\rangle\):
\[
    E_2 = \sum_{n,m} \frac{\langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \phi_{n,m} \rangle \langle \phi_{n,m} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle}{E_0^{(\text{unp.})} - E_{n,m}^{(\text{unp.})}}.
\]
Out of this seemingly infinite sum over all basis states, only highly specific terms survive the projection. The number of electrons \(n\) in the intermediate state must strictly remain equal to \(n_e\), and the number of phonons \(m\) must be exactly \(n_{ph} + 1\) or \(n_{ph} - 1\). This selection rule allows us to simplify the numerator structure using Kronecker deltas:
\[
    E_2 = \sum_{n,m} \frac{\delta_{n,n_e} \left( \delta_{m,n_{ph}+1} + \delta_{m,n_{ph}-1} \right)}{E_0^{(\text{unp.})} - E_{n,m}^{(\text{unp.})}}.
\]

\begin{example}[Single electron application]
    To intimately understand the underlying physics, let us perform the explicit calculation for the simplest possible case: a single electron with initial momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) and spin \(\sigma\), and exactly zero initial phonons (\(n_e = 1\), \(n_{ph} = 0\)). The initial state is constructed as \(|\phi_0\rangle = c_{\mathbf{p},\sigma}^\dagger |0\rangle\). Applying the interaction \(V_1\) to this specific state and carefully enforcing both momentum and spin conservation through the resulting delta functions, we obtain:
    \[
        V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle = \sum_{\mathbf{q},\sigma'} \sum_{\mathbf{k}'} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{-\mathbf{q}}) c_{\mathbf{k}'+\mathbf{q},\sigma'}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}',\sigma'} c_{\mathbf{p},\sigma}^\dagger |0\rangle
    \]
    \[
        = \sum_{\mathbf{q},\sigma'} \sum_{\mathbf{k}'} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{-\mathbf{q}}) c_{\mathbf{k}'+\mathbf{q},\sigma'}^\dagger \delta_{\sigma,\sigma'} \delta_{\mathbf{k}',\mathbf{p}} |0\rangle
    \]
    \[
        = \sum_{\mathbf{q}} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{-\mathbf{q}}) c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\sigma}^\dagger |0\rangle.
    \]
    This rigorous evaluation explicitly shows the initial electron's momentum being shifted by \(\mathbf{q}\) due to the interaction with the phonon field.

    Now, we compute the quantum overlap of this resulting state with a generic intermediate state \(|\phi_{n,m}\rangle\). For a non-zero overlap to exist, the intermediate state must contain exactly 1 electron and 1 phonon (\(n = 1\), \(m = 1\)), meaning its bra form is constructed as \(\langle \phi_{n,m} | = \langle 0 | c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}',\sigma'} a_{\mathbf{q}'}\). Taking the inner product systematically isolates the exact phonon mode that was emitted:
    \[
        \langle \phi_{n,m} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle = \left( \sum_{\mathbf{q}',\sigma'} \langle 0 | c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}',\sigma'} a_{\mathbf{q}'} \right) \left( \sum_{\mathbf{q}} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{-\mathbf{q}}) c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\sigma}^\dagger |0\rangle \right)
    \]
    \[
        = \sum_{\sigma',\mathbf{q}'} \delta_{\sigma,\sigma'} \delta_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}'} M_{\mathbf{q}} = M_{\mathbf{q}}.
    \]

    Finally, substituting this squared matrix element back into the formal second-order energy formula, we must evaluate the corresponding energy denominator. The initial unperturbed energy is simply the kinetic energy of the single electron, \(E_0^{(\text{unp.})} = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}\), and the intermediate unperturbed energy includes both the shifted electron and the newly emitted phonon, \(E_{n,m}^{(\text{unp.})} = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} + \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}\). Thus, the second-order correction is:
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:single_electron_shift}
        E_2 = \frac{M_{\mathbf{q}}^2}{-\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}} + (\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}})}.
    \end{equation}
    This crucial formula explicitly defines the effective energy shift that the virtual phonon cloud induces on a single isolated fermion.
\end{example}

\subsection{Two Body Problem}

To rigorously study the effective electron-electron interactions mediated by phonons, we must transition from a single-particle picture to an initial unperturbed state \( |\phi_0\rangle \) containing at least two particles. We define this two-fermion ground state as:
\[
    |\phi_0\rangle = c_{\mathbf{p},\alpha}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}',\alpha'}^\dagger |0\rangle.
\]

As established previously, the first-order perturbation theory correction trivially vanishes because the interaction Hamiltonian strictly creates or destroys a single phonon, ensuring orthogonality with the initial phonon-free state. We therefore proceed directly to evaluate the second-order energy correction, which requires computing the transition matrix elements connecting our initial two-particle state to the virtual intermediate states:
\[
    \langle \phi_{n,m} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle \quad \text{and} \quad \langle \phi_{2,1} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle.
\]
Applying the electron-phonon interaction operator \( V_1 \) to our initial state allows the fermionic destruction operator \( c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} \) within \( V_1 \) to contract with either of the two initial electrons (possessing momenta \( \mathbf{p} \) or \( \mathbf{p}' \)):
\[
    V_1 |\phi_0\rangle = \sum_{\mathbf{q}} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{-\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{\mathbf{q}}) \sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} c_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} c_{\mathbf{p},\alpha}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}',\alpha'}^\dagger |0\rangle
\]
\[
    = \sum_{\mathbf{q}} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{-\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{\mathbf{q}}) \left( c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\alpha}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}',\alpha'}^\dagger + c_{\mathbf{p},\alpha}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}'+\mathbf{q},\alpha'}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle.
\]

These two diagrams correspond precisely to the two possible intermediate states containing exactly one virtual phonon, which can be emitted by either the first electron or the second electron. The bra forms of these specific intermediate states that yield a non-zero overlap are given by:
\[
    \langle \phi_{2,1} | = \langle 0 | a_{-\mathbf{q}} c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\alpha} c_{\mathbf{p}',\alpha'},
\]
\[
    \langle \tilde{\phi}_{2,1} | = \langle 0 | a_{\mathbf{q}} c_{\mathbf{p},\alpha} c_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q},\alpha'}.
\]

We now substitute these transition matrix elements into the standard second-order perturbation formula. The expectation values of the paired creation and annihilation operators simply trace out to unity, allowing us to factor out the squared electron-phonon matrix element \( M_{\mathbf{q}}^2 \):
\[
    E_2' = \left[ \frac{\langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \phi_{2,1} \rangle \langle \phi_{2,1} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle}{E_0 - E_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})}} + \frac{\langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \tilde{\phi}_{2,1} \rangle \langle \tilde{\phi}_{2,1} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle}{E_0 - \tilde{E}_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})}} \right]
\]
\[
    = M_{\mathbf{q}}^2 \left( \frac{1}{E_0 - E_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})}} + \frac{1}{E_0 - \tilde{E}_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})}} \right).
\]
By evaluating this algebraic sum, we can determine whether the resulting energy shift represents a repulsive or an attractive effective interaction between the electrons, a property completely dictated by the sign of the unperturbed energy denominators.

Defining the initial unperturbed energy and the intermediate unperturbed energies for these two distinct exchange processes, we have:
\[
    E_0 = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} + \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'},
\]
\[
    E_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})} = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} + \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'} + \hbar\omega_{-\mathbf{q}},
\]
\[
    \tilde{E}_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})} = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q}} + \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} + \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}.
\]
Substituting these explicit energies into our second-order expression, the energy shift \( E_2' \) becomes:
\[
    E_2' = M_{\mathbf{q}}^2 \left( \frac{1}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} - \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}} + \frac{1}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q}} - \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}} \right).
\]
To simplify this result, we apply the physical limit of a small momentum exchange \( \mathbf{q} \). We assume the relevant scattering processes keep the electrons tightly confined close to the Fermi surface (within the heavily populated Fermi sea), which imposes the approximate kinematic relation:
\[
    \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} \simeq \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'}.
\]
Applying this small-\( \mathbf{q} \) approximation, the effective energy shift neatly reduces to the Fröhlich interaction form:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:frohlich_effective_interaction}
    E_2' \simeq M_{\mathbf{q}}^2 \left( \frac{2\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}}{(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}})^2 - (\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}})^2} \right).
\end{equation}
This pivotal result reveals that the effective phonon-mediated interaction can dynamically manifest as either attractive or repulsive, depending entirely on the sign of the energy denominator.

\subsubsection{Attractive Interaction and Isotope Effect}

We now restrict our focus to the low-temperature regime, specifically when \( T \ll T_D \), where \( T_D \sim \omega_D \) is the characteristic Debye temperature of the crystal lattice. In this regime, the electrons lack the thermal energy necessary to scatter far from the Fermi surface. Consequently, the kinetic energy difference between the initial and scattered electron states is constrained to be much smaller than the typical phonon energy (the Debye energy):
\[
    |\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}| \ll \hbar\omega_D.
\]
Under this stringent condition, the denominator in Equation \ref{eq:frohlich_effective_interaction} is overwhelmingly dominated by the phonon energy squared term, \( -(\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}})^2 \), ensuring it naturally becomes negative. The effective interaction therefore simplifies to a constant, weakly attractive potential:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:attractive_potential_approx}
    E_2' \simeq \frac{-|M_{\mathbf{q}}|^2}{\hbar\omega_D}.
\end{equation}

Recall from our fundamental coupling definitions that the squared electron-phonon matrix element, \( |M_{\mathbf{q}}|^2 \), is inversely proportional to the mass of the lattice ions (\( M_{\text{ion}} \)). Therefore, the overall strength of the attractive interaction scales directly as:
\[
    E_2' \propto \frac{1}{M_{\text{ion}}}.
\]
This simple inverse proportionality provides a profound microscopic explanation for the experimentally observed \textbf{isotope effect} in conventional superconductors, where the critical transition temperature is found to vary predictably with the specific mass of the isotopes that comprise the crystal lattice.

\begin{remark}
    While our derivation treats \( V_1 \) as a small perturbation within a simplified cubic lattice, this is not always universally applicable. Because the matrix element \( M_{\mathbf{q}} \) scales inversely with the ion mass, the effective electron-phonon interaction can become exceedingly strong in materials composed of very light ions. In such strong-coupling regimes, the perturbative approach breaks down, necessitating more advanced non-perturbative methods (such as Eliashberg theory). Furthermore, real materials often possess complex crystal geometries far beyond a simple cubic structure; these specific geometric details fundamentally alter the phonon spectrum and, consequently, the precise structure of the effective electron-electron interaction.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{The BCS Effective Potential}

For the specific purposes of successfully developing the macroscopic BCS theory (Step 1 of the paradigm), we treat this phonon-mediated interaction as a separable, strictly attractive potential. We completely drop its explicit momentum dependence in favor of a constant effective coupling strength, phenomenologically denoted as \( -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \).

Furthermore, while the complete interaction Hamiltonian realistically includes the residual screened Coulomb repulsion (the Thomas-Fermi term), the fundamental assumption of BCS theory is that the attractive phonon-mediated part robustly dominates for electrons near the Fermi surface. Thus, we neglect the residual electrostatic repulsion and approximate the total effective electron-electron potential as purely attractive:
\[
    V_{e-e,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}'} \simeq -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 + \cancel{V_{\text{Thomas-Fermi}}} \simeq -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2.
\]

It is crucial to emphasize that while this attractive energy shift \( \Delta E = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \) (derived by discarding scattering processes with energy exchanges far beyond the Debye cutoff \( \omega_D \)) is exceedingly small, it is fundamentally sufficient to destabilize the unperturbed Fermi sea and induce the formation of a bound state between two electrons. Having established the scalar amplitude of this attractive potential, the immediate next step is to rigorously construct the full operatorial part of the interaction Hamiltonian to be utilized in solving the macroscopic many-body problem.





We are assuming weak interactions, but that is not always the case: the coefficient \(M(\mathbf{q})\) is inversely proportional to the mass of the ions in the lattice, and thus the interaction can be very strong in the case of light ions. In this case, we need to use a non-perturbative approach.

One has to pay attention also to the geometry of the lattice, since our treatment was based on a cubic lattice, but in general the lattice can be more complicated. The geometry of the lattice can affect the phonon spectrum and thus the effective interaction between electrons.

We got \(\Delta E = - \vert g_{eff} \vert^2 \) for phonons with \(\omega < \omega_D\) (discarding processes going to far away from the Fermi surface). This is a very small energy, but it is enough to create a bound state of two electrons. Now we have to find the operatorial part of the Hamiltonian, which is the part that will be used in the many-body problem.

\subsection{Effective Hamiltonian and Two-Body Ground State}

In the second quantization formalism, the interacting part of the many-body Hamiltonian, $H_{\text{int}}$, capturing this fundamental scattering process between pairs of fermions, can be modeled as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bcs_interaction_hamiltonian}
    H_{\text{int}} = \frac{1}{2V} \sum_{\mathbf{k}, \mathbf{k}', \mathbf{q}} \sum_{\sigma, \sigma'} V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) c_{\mathbf{k}' + \frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}' + \frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}, \sigma'}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k} + \frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}, \sigma'} c_{\mathbf{k} + \frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}, \sigma},
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the macroscopic volume of the system, and $c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}$ and $c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}^\dagger$ are respectively the fermionic annihilation and creation operators for an electron possessing momentum $\mathbf{k}$ and spin projection $\sigma$. The scattering amplitude is governed by the matrix element $V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k})$. We are summing over all possible incoming and outgoing momenta (and also over all feasible values of $\mathbf{q}$), as well as over the spin indices $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$. The factor of \(1/2\) is included to avoid double counting of identical scattering processes.

Notice how phonons have disappeared from the final expression for the interaction Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:bcs_interaction_hamiltonian}. This is because we have integrated out the phonon degrees of freedom, leaving only an effective interaction between the electrons. The phonons are still present in the system, but they are now treated as virtual excitations that mediate the interaction between the electrons. Our goal is now to ``solve'' the many-body problem of interacting fermions with this effective interaction, which is a highly non-trivial task. Thus we will limit our analysis to the simplest possible model that still captures the essential physics of the problem: the ground state of a system with 2 electrons interacting via the effective potential \( V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) \) in the presence of a filled Fermi sea of non-interacting electrons. This is the original problem solved by Cooper, and it is the starting point for the BCS theory.

We are then trying to compute the following wavefunction in the center of mass frame:
\[
    \Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \sigma_1; \mathbf{r}_2, \sigma_2) = e^{i\mathbf{k}_{cm} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{cm}} \varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) \phi(\sigma_1, \sigma_2),
\]
where we assumed translational invariance and separated the center of mass motion from the relative motion. We were able to factorize the wavefunction into a plane wave for the center of mass motion, a spatial wavefunction for the relative motion, and a spin wavefunction, since in the hamiltonian the spin and spatial degrees of freedom are decoupled (but there may be cases in which we have some sort of spin-orbit coupling, which would couple the spin and spatial degrees of freedom).

Considering the spin wavefunction, we have two possibilities: the singlet state (antisymmetric) and the triplet state (symmetric). The Pauli exclusion principle requires that if we are requiring local pairing in the same state, then we can only accept states like \(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\) and \(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\) (plus linear combinations). Thus we have
\[
    \text{- singlet:}\quad \phi(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\ket{\uparrow\downarrow} - \ket{\downarrow\uparrow}),
\]
\[
    \text{- triplet:}\quad \phi(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\ket{\uparrow\downarrow} + \ket{\downarrow\uparrow}),
\]
but then the triplet is unfavorable by energy considerations...

We now what the function to be antisymmetric for exchange of the two electrons, and the spin part is antisymmetric, so the spatial part must be symmetric. This means that the spatial wavefunction must depend only upon the distance between the two electrons, \(\varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) = \varphi(r)\). Thus the problem now depends only on the relative distance between the two electrons, which is a simpler treatment. Then we can expand in Fourier modes the spatial wavefunction:
\[
    \varphi(r) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)}.
\]
and we also need to require that \(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}} = \varphi_{-\mathbf{k}}\) because the spatial wavefunction is symmetric. We can then write the Schrödinger equation for the relative motion of the two electrons in momentum space:
\[
    E \Psi = (H_0 + H_{int}) \Psi, \quad \Psi = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \phi(\sigma_1, \sigma_2),
\]

In order to find the functional form of the wavefunction \(\varphi\), we need to compute the following matrix element:
\[
    \bra{\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}} H \ket{\Psi},
\]
where \(\ket{\Psi} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} \ket{\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}}\) and
\[
    \ket{\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( c^\dagger_{\mathbf{k} \uparrow} c^\dagger_{-\mathbf{k} \downarrow} -  c^\dagger_{-\mathbf{k} \uparrow} c^\dagger_{\mathbf{k} \downarrow} \right) \ket{0}.
\]

Now we split the computation into two parts: the free non interacting part and the interaction part. The kinetic part is straightforward:
\[
    H_0 \ket{\Psi} = \left( \frac{\hat{p}_1^2}{2m} + \frac{\hat{p}_2^2}{2m} \right) \left( \sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \phi(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) \right) = 2 \sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime} \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \phi(\sigma_1, \sigma_2).

For the interaction part, we have to compute the following:
\[

which after some algebra and the summations resolved over the deltas, we get:

\subsection{The Effective Attractive Potential}

To capture the essential physics of the Fröhlich mechanism, specifically that the phonon-mediated attraction only overcomes Coulomb repulsion for electrons energetically close to the Fermi surface, the effective attractive potential is explicitly modeled by a piecewise function:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bcs_potential}
    V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) = \begin{cases}
        -g_{\text{e,ph}}^2 & \text{if } |\epsilon_k - \mu| < \hbar\omega_D \text{ and } |\epsilon_{k'} - \mu| < \hbar\omega_D, \\
        0                  & \text{elsewhere,}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $g_{\text{e,ph}}$ represents the effective coupling constant between the electrons and phonons, $\mu$ is the chemical potential of the electron gas (recalling that in the low-temperature limit $T \to 0$, the chemical potential intimately approaches the Fermi energy, $\mu(T) \xrightarrow{T \to 0} E_F$), $\epsilon_k$ is the non-interacting kinetic energy of the fermion, and $\omega_D$ is the characteristic Debye frequency of the ionic lattice.

By analyzing the explicit energy bounds in the expression for the potential \eqref{eq:bcs_potential}, we clearly see a profound physical restriction: only those electrons located within a remarkably narrow energy shell surrounding the Fermi sphere are permitted to interact attractively with each other.

To express this restricted interaction more compactly, it is highly advantageous to introduce a dimensionless cut-off function $w_k = \Theta(\hbar\omega_D - |\epsilon_k - \mu|)$, where $\Theta$ denotes the standard Heaviside step function. Utilizing this notation, the piecewise potential \eqref{eq:bcs_potential} can be elegantly rewritten in a strictly factorized form:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bcs_potential_separable}
    V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) = -g_{\text{e,ph}}^2 w_k w_{k'}.
\end{equation}
This mathematically represents the localized attractive potential acting exclusively between fermions scattered around the Fermi energy in a superconductor. Note that this specific theoretical form of the potential is entirely \emph{separable}; the dependence on the incoming momentum $\mathbf{k}$ and the outgoing momentum $\mathbf{k}'$ factors into independent terms, a property that will dramatically simplify the subsequent many-body calculations.

\subsection{Ultracold Fermi Gases}

As previously mentioned, the BCS theoretical framework can also be employed to study systems of ultracold Fermi atoms. In these highly controlled systems, the effective interparticle interaction possesses a very short range compared to the average interparticle distance. Because of this separation of scales, it is highly convenient to model the interaction using a separable potential in momentum space:
\[
    V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) = v_0 w_k w_{k'},
\]
where $w_k = \Theta(k_0 - k)$ utilizes the Heaviside step function to introduce a strict ultraviolet momentum cutoff $k_0$, and $v_0$ represents the bare interaction strength. Note that formally, this separable potential is structurally very similar to the one utilized for modeling electron-phonon interactions in superconductors.

For a genuinely short-range potential in position space, the corresponding cutoff in momentum space must be taken to be very large, meaning $k_0 \to \infty$. We thus need to understand how to appropriately treat this divergent limit mathematically. The core physical idea is that as the cutoff $k_0$ goes to infinity, the bare interaction parameter $v_0$ must simultaneously change with $k_0$ in a precise manner in order to keep the physically observable scattering length $a$ strictly constant.

To rigorously establish this relationship, we invoke the quantum theory of scattering.\footnote{For a comprehensive treatment, see, e.g., John R. Taylor, \textit{Scattering Theory: The Quantum Theory of Nonrelativistic Collisions} (Dover Books).} We know that the elastic scattering amplitude $f(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) \equiv f(\mathbf{k}' \leftarrow \mathbf{k})$ for scattering from an initial momentum $\mathbf{k}$ to a final momentum $\mathbf{k}'$ can be obtained directly from the T-matrix, $T(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}, z)$. This T-matrix satisfies the fundamental Lippmann-Schwinger integral equation:
\[
    T(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}, z) = V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) + \int \frac{d^3k''}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}'')}{z - \frac{(k'')^2}{2m_r}} T(\mathbf{k}'', \mathbf{k}, z),
\]
where $m_r$ is the reduced mass of the colliding two-body system. For a gas of identical fermions of mass $m$, the reduced mass is simply $m_r = m/2$. The formal relation linking the T-matrix to the physical scattering amplitude is evaluated on-shell:
\[
    f(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) = -\frac{m_r}{2\pi\hbar^2} T\left(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}, z = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m_r} + i0^+\right),
\]
where $i0^+$ is an infinitesimal positive imaginary term. In the extreme low-energy limit, the scattering amplitude limits to the negative of the scattering length, implying that:
\[
    T(0,0,0) = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m_r} a.
\]
We can safely ignore the infinitesimal term $i0^+$ because it becomes entirely irrelevant for the principal value of the integral when evaluating at exactly $z = 0$.

Because our proposed potential is purely separable and takes the form $V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) = v_0 w_k w_{k'}$, the integral equation can be easily solved analytically. Substituting the potential yields:
\[
    T(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}, z) = v_0 w_k w_{k'} + v_0 w_{k'} \int \frac{d^3k''}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{w_{k''}}{z - \frac{(k'')^2}{2m_r}} T(\mathbf{k}'', \mathbf{k}, z).
\]
By employing the separable ansatz $T(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}, z) = T(z) w_k w_{k'}$, we factor out the momentum dependence and obtain a simple algebraic equation for the scalar function $T(z)$:
\[
    T(z) = v_0 + v_0 \int \frac{d^3k''}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{w_{k''}^2}{z - \frac{(k'')^2}{2m_r}} T(z).
\]
This equation is readily solved for $T(z)$, yielding:
\[
    T(z) = \frac{v_0}{1 - v_0 \int \frac{d^3k''}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{w_{k''}^2}{z - \frac{(k'')^2}{2m_r}}}.
\]
Setting $z=0$ to connect with our low-energy scattering length boundary condition provides:
\[
    T(0) = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{v_0} + \int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{w_k^2}{\frac{k^2}{2m_r}}}.
\]
Equating this result to the previously derived kinematic definition $T(0,0,0) = \frac{4\pi\hbar^2}{m} a$ (where we have substituted $m_r = m/2$), we obtain:
\[
    \frac{4\pi\hbar^2}{m} a = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{v_0} + \int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{w_k^2}{\frac{k^2}{2m_r}}}.
\]
By isolating the inverse bare coupling strength, we can rewrite this relation as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bare_coupling_renormalization}
    \frac{1}{v_0} = \frac{m}{4\pi\hbar^2 a} - \int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{w_k^2}{2\epsilon_k},
\end{equation}
where we have explicitly used the free-particle kinetic energy dispersion $\epsilon_k = \hbar^2 k^2 / 2m$. Equation \ref{eq:bare_coupling_renormalization} reveals how the bare interaction strength $v_0$ must scale intricately with the ultraviolet momentum cutoff $k_0$ (which is embedded within the definition of $w_k$) when the macroscopic scattering length $a$ is held strictly fixed. This mathematical behavior is deeply analogous to a running coupling constant in quantum field theory. It is critical to note that Equation \ref{eq:bare_coupling_renormalization} is universally valid for any generic separable potential, independent of the specific functional form chosen for the cutoff $w_k$.

For our specific hard-sphere cutoff model, $w_k = \Theta(k_0 - k)$, the integral can be evaluated analytically in spherical coordinates, yielding:
\[
    v_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{m} \frac{1}{\frac{1}{4\pi a} - \frac{k_0}{2\pi^2}}.
\]
To analyze the physics of a true zero-range contact interaction, we take the limit as the cutoff $k_0 \to \infty$ while holding $a$ constant (such that the dimensionless parameter $k_0 a \gg 1$). Expanding the expression in this limit gives:
\[
    v_0 = -\frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2}{m k_0} \left[ 1 + \frac{\pi}{2k_0 a} \right].
\]
This asymptotic expansion clearly demonstrates that in the limiting case of a perfectly localized contact potential, the bare coupling strength must approach zero from below, $v_0 \to 0^-$, as $k_0 \to \infty$. Notice also that the physical scattering length $a$ appears exclusively in the subleading term on the right-hand side of this limiting expression.

\section{Grand Canonical Hamiltonian and Ground State}

Let us now proceed to study the many-body problem. We will work within the grand canonical ensemble, where the thermodynamic properties of the system are governed by the partition function \( \mathcal{Z} \), defined as:
\[
    \mathcal{Z} = \text{Tr}\left\{e^{-\beta \hat{K}}\right\},
\]
where \( \hat{K} = \hat{H} - \mu \hat{N} \) is the grand canonical "Hamiltonian", \( \mu \) is the chemical potential, and \( \hat{N} \) is the fermionic number operator. Using the standard second-quantized expression for the interaction, \( \hat{K} \) takes the form:
\[
    \hat{K} = \sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} \xi_k c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} + \frac{1}{2V} \sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}',\mathbf{q}} \sum_{\sigma,\sigma'} V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) c_{\mathbf{k}'+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\sigma}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}'+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\sigma'}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\sigma'} c_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\sigma},
\]
where we have defined the single-particle kinetic energy relative to the chemical potential as \( \xi_k = \epsilon_k - \mu \). For notational convenience, we will write the non-interacting kinetic part of \( \hat{K} \) as \( \hat{T} \).

We are fundamentally interested in the pairing mechanism between fermions of opposite spin (spin up \( \uparrow \) and spin down \( \downarrow \)), as this is the dominant process in most conventional superconductors. The interaction between identical fermions (same spin) is irrelevant for the pairing mechanism and can thus be safely ignored, since it exerts only a negligible effect on the physical properties of a superconductor. Furthermore, in the specific case of ultracold Fermi gases, the effective interaction is strictly short-ranged, meaning only interactions between up and down fermions need to be considered; the presence of two identical fermions in the same spatial region is strongly suppressed by the Pauli exclusion principle. By restricting the interaction term to couple only opposite spins, and performing the summation over the spin indices \( \sigma \) and \( \sigma' \), we obtain:
\[
    \hat{K} = \hat{T} + \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}',\mathbf{q}} V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) c_{\mathbf{k}'+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}'+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\downarrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\downarrow} c_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\uparrow}.
\]

\subsection{Mean-Field Approximation}

We now proceed in a manner conceptually similar to the treatment of the weakly-interacting Bose gas, with the primary difference being that in the present fermionic case, we anticipate the macroscopic condensation of \emph{pairs} of fermions rather than individual particles. Consider the pair destruction operator factor \( c_{-\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\downarrow} c_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\uparrow} \) embedded in the interaction term. We rewrite it (keeping all momentum and spin indices implicit for brevity) by expanding around its thermodynamic mean:
\[
    cc = cc - \langle cc \rangle + \langle cc \rangle = \delta(cc) + \langle cc \rangle,
\]
where \( \delta(cc) = cc - \langle cc \rangle \) represents the deviation (or fluctuation) of the operator \( cc \) from its expectation value \( \langle cc \rangle \). We will assume this deviation to be a small quantity. The exact same decomposition is applied to the pair creation factor \( c^\dagger c^\dagger \). Substituting these back into the interaction and systematically neglecting the second-order fluctuation term of order \( \delta(c^\dagger c^\dagger)\delta(cc) \), the four-operator interaction is linearized into a bilinear form:
\[
    c^\dagger c^\dagger cc = \delta(c^\dagger c^\dagger) \langle cc \rangle + \langle c^\dagger c^\dagger \rangle \delta(cc) + \langle c^\dagger c^\dagger \rangle \langle cc \rangle
\]
\[
    = c^\dagger c^\dagger \langle cc \rangle + \langle c^\dagger c^\dagger \rangle cc - \langle c^\dagger c^\dagger \rangle \langle cc \rangle.
\]

Because the macroscopic condensation of pairs is physically expected to occur exactly at zero center-of-mass momentum, the expectation values are non-zero only for \( \mathbf{q} = 0 \). Thus, we can rigorously set:
\[
    \langle c_{-\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\downarrow} c_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\uparrow} \rangle = \delta_{\mathbf{q},0} A_{\mathbf{k}},
\]
\[
    \langle c_{\mathbf{k}'+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}'+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\downarrow}^\dagger \rangle = \delta_{\mathbf{q},0} A_{\mathbf{k}'}^*,
\]
where \( A_{\mathbf{k}} \) is a function of the momentum \( \mathbf{k} \) that remains to be determined. Substituting this mean-field ansatz, the grand canonical Hamiltonian simplifies to:
\[
    \hat{K} = \hat{T} + \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}'} V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) \left[ c_{\mathbf{k}',\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}',\downarrow}^\dagger A_{\mathbf{k}} + c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} A_{\mathbf{k}'}^* \right] - \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}'} V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) A_{\mathbf{k}'}^* A_{\mathbf{k}}.
\]

To render this expression more physically transparent, we define the momentum-dependent superconducting gap parameter \( \Delta_{\mathbf{k}} \) and its complex conjugate:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gap_definition}
    \Delta_{\mathbf{k}'} = - \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) A_{\mathbf{k}}, \quad \Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^* = - \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k}'} V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) A_{\mathbf{k}'}^*.
\end{equation}
Note that these definitions are perfectly consistent because any time-reversal invariant potential satisfies the symmetry \( V(\mathbf{k}, \mathbf{k}') = V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k})^* \). With these definitions, one obtains a bilinear Hamiltonian:
\[
    \hat{K} = \hat{T} - \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \left[ \Delta_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger + \Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^* c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \right] + \sum_{\mathbf{k}} A_{\mathbf{k}}^* \Delta_{\mathbf{k}}.
\]

\subsection{Diagonalization of the Hamiltonian}

To elegantly diagonalize this bilinear Hamiltonian, it is highly convenient to express it in a matrix representation using Nambu spinors. By leveraging the fundamental fermionic anti-commutation relations, we can rewrite the kinetic energy term for the spin-down particles as \( \xi_k c_{\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k},\downarrow} = \xi_k - \xi_k c_{\mathbf{k},\downarrow} c_{\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \). This algebraic maneuver allows us to recast the operator \( \hat{K} \) into the following compact matrix form:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:nambu_hamiltonian}
    \hat{K} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \begin{pmatrix} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger & c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \xi_k & -\Delta_{\mathbf{k}} \\ -\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^* & -\xi_k \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \\ c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix} + C,
\end{equation}
where the constant energy offset \( C \) is explicitly given by:
\[
    C = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \left( \xi_k + A_{\mathbf{k}}^* \Delta_{\mathbf{k}} \right).
\]
Let us define the central \( 2 \times 2 \) Hermitian matrix \( M_{\mathbf{k}} \) as:
\[
    M_{\mathbf{k}} = \begin{pmatrix} \xi_k & -\Delta_{\mathbf{k}} \\ -\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^* & -\xi_k \end{pmatrix}.
\]

We now show that the many-body problem of diagonalizing the quadratic form \( \hat{K} \) reduces entirely to the problem of diagonalizing the independent \( 2 \times 2 \) matrix \( M_{\mathbf{k}} \) for every momentum mode \( \mathbf{k} \). In strict analogy to the procedure utilized for the weakly interacting Bose gas, we seek a transformation to a new set of fermionic creation and destruction operators, \( \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} \) and \( \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}^\dagger \), that diagonalize \( \hat{K} \). We introduce a transformation matrix \( U_{\mathbf{k}} \) such that:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bogoliubov_transform}
    \begin{pmatrix} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \\ c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix} = U_{\mathbf{k}} \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \\ \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix}, \quad \begin{pmatrix} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger & c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger & \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} \end{pmatrix} U_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger.
\end{equation}
This transformation must simultaneously satisfy two critical conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The terms appearing in the grand canonical Hamiltonian, once expressed in terms of the new operators, must form a diagonal matrix \( D_{\mathbf{k}} \):
          \[
              \begin{pmatrix} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger & c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} \end{pmatrix} M_{\mathbf{k}} \begin{pmatrix} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \\ c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger & \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} \end{pmatrix} U_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger M_{\mathbf{k}} U_{\mathbf{k}} \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \\ \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix},
          \]
          with \( D_{\mathbf{k}} = U_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger M_{\mathbf{k}} U_{\mathbf{k}} \) being explicitly diagonal.
    \item The fundamental anti-commutation rules must be preserved for the new quasiparticle operators, ensuring in particular that:
          \[
              \{ \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}, \gamma_{\mathbf{k}',\sigma'}^\dagger \} = \delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}'} \delta_{\sigma,\sigma'},
          \]
          with all remaining anti-commutators identically zero.
\end{enumerate}

We can readily prove that requiring the transformation \( U_{\mathbf{k}} \) to be unitary guarantees the preservation of these anti-commutation relations on the new operators. Let us parameterize the inverse transformation matrix as:
\[
    U_{\mathbf{k}}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The relation linking the bare \( c \) operators to the quasiparticle \( \gamma \) operators is then:
\[
    \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \\ \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix} = U_{\mathbf{k}}^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \\ c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix}.
\]
By explicitly substituting this into the anti-commutators and using the known relations for the bare \( c \) operators, it is straightforward to check that:
\[
    \{ \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}, \gamma_{\mathbf{k}',\uparrow}^\dagger \} = (|a_{11}|^2 + |a_{12}|^2) \delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}'},
\]
\[
    \{ \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}, \gamma_{-\mathbf{k}',\downarrow}^\dagger \} = (|a_{21}|^2 + |a_{22}|^2) \delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}'},
\]
\[
    \{ \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}, \gamma_{-\mathbf{k}',\downarrow} \} = (a_{11} a_{21}^* + a_{12} a_{22}^*) \delta_{\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}'}.
\]
In order to successfully satisfy the fermionic algebra, the coefficients must obey:
\[
    1 = |a_{11}|^2 + |a_{12}|^2,
\]
\[
    1 = |a_{21}|^2 + |a_{22}|^2,
\]
\[
    0 = a_{11} a_{21}^* + a_{12} a_{22}^*.
\]
These three constraints are automatically and exactly satisfied by unitary matrices. This implies that \( U_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger M_{\mathbf{k}} U_{\mathbf{k}} = U_{\mathbf{k}}^{-1} M_{\mathbf{k}} U_{\mathbf{k}} \); thus, finding a unitary matrix that diagonalizes the Hermitian matrix \( M_{\mathbf{k}} \) will successfully diagonalize the entire many-body Hamiltonian \( \hat{K} \).

The eigenvalues \( \lambda \) of \( M_{\mathbf{k}} \) are determined by its characteristic equation:
\[
    (\xi_k - \lambda)(-\xi_k - \lambda) - |\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^2 = 0,
\]
from which we immediately obtain the roots \( \lambda = \pm E_{\mathbf{k}} \), where:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:quasiparticle_dispersion}
    E_{\mathbf{k}} = \sqrt{\xi_k^2 + |\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^2} > 0.
\end{equation}
This fundamentally important quantity \( E_{\mathbf{k}} \) represents the energy dispersion of the superconducting quasiparticle excitations.

The normalized eigenvector of \( M_{\mathbf{k}} \) corresponding to the positive eigenvalue \( E_{\mathbf{k}} \) takes the form:
\[
    \begin{pmatrix} u_{\mathbf{k}}^* \\ -v_{\mathbf{k}}^* \end{pmatrix},
\]
with the coherence factors defined as:
\[
    u_{\mathbf{k}}^* = \frac{\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf{k}}(E_{\mathbf{k}} - \xi_k)}}, \quad v_{\mathbf{k}}^* = \frac{E_{\mathbf{k}} - \xi_k}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf{k}}(E_{\mathbf{k}} - \xi_k)}} = v_{\mathbf{k}}.
\]
Conversely, the normalized eigenvector corresponding to the negative eigenvalue \( -E_{\mathbf{k}} \) is:
\[
    \begin{pmatrix} v_{\mathbf{k}} \\ u_{\mathbf{k}} \end{pmatrix}.
\]
\begin{remark}
    In defining these eigenvectors, a specific choice of phase factors has been made such that \( v_{\mathbf{k}}^* \) is strictly real and the determinant of the transformation matrix is exactly \( \det U_{\mathbf{k}} = 1 \).
\end{remark}
Using these two orthogonal eigenvectors, one may immediately construct the explicit unitary transformation \( U_{\mathbf{k}} \) that diagonalizes \( M_{\mathbf{k}} \):
\[
    U_{\mathbf{k}} = \begin{pmatrix} u_{\mathbf{k}}^* & v_{\mathbf{k}} \\ -v_{\mathbf{k}}^* & u_{\mathbf{k}} \end{pmatrix},
\]
with its corresponding inverse:
\[
    U_{\mathbf{k}}^{-1} = U_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} u_{\mathbf{k}} & -v_{\mathbf{k}} \\ v_{\mathbf{k}}^* & u_{\mathbf{k}}^* \end{pmatrix}.
\]

By applying this inverse transformation, we obtain the explicit mathematical expressions for the new operators in terms of the old ones (the celebrated Bogoliubov transformation):
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bogoliubov_operators}
    \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} = u_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} - v_{\mathbf{k}} c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger,
\end{equation}
\[
    \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger = v_{\mathbf{k}}^* c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} + u_{\mathbf{k}}^* c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger.
\]
Notice that the new annihilation operator \( \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \) physically mixes the old creation operator \( c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \) and destruction operator \( c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \), specifically entangling particles with opposite spin and momentum. (Equivalently, taking the Hermitian adjoint of the second equation yields \( \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} = u_{\mathbf{k}} c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} + v_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger \)).

Expressed in terms of these newly defined \( \gamma \) operators, the grand canonical Hamiltonian assumes its fully diagonalized form:
\[
    \hat{K} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger & \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} \end{pmatrix} D_{\mathbf{k}} \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \\ \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix} + C,
\]
where the diagonal eigenvalue matrix \( D_{\mathbf{k}} = U_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger M_{\mathbf{k}} U_{\mathbf{k}} \) is given by:
\[
    D_{\mathbf{k}} = \begin{pmatrix} E_{\mathbf{k}} & 0 \\ 0 & -E_{\mathbf{k}} \end{pmatrix}.
\]
By explicitly expanding this matrix product and once again applying the quasiparticle anti-commutation relations, we obtain:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:diagonalized_canonical_hamiltonian}
    \hat{K} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \left( E_{\mathbf{k}} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} - E_{\mathbf{k}} \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow} \gamma_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \right) + C = \sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} E_{\mathbf{k}} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}^\dagger \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} + C',
\end{equation}
where the newly defined constant term \( C' \) absorbs the commutation shifts and is explicitly given by:
\[
    C' = C - \sum_{\mathbf{k}} E_{\mathbf{k}} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \left( \xi_k + A_{\mathbf{k}}^* \Delta_{\mathbf{k}} - E_{\mathbf{k}} \right).
\]
Because the quasiparticle energy dispersion \( E_{\mathbf{k}} \) is strictly positive, the operator \( \sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} E_{\mathbf{k}} \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}^\dagger \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} \) is positive semi-definite. Consequently, the constant \( C' \) physically represents the lowest possible eigenvalue of \( \hat{K} \), which corresponds exactly to the ground state grand potential of the superconducting system.

\subsection{Ground State Wave Function}

In the grand-canonical ensemble, the thermodynamic thermal average of any physical operator \( \hat{O} \) is given by the standard statistical mechanics trace formula:
\[
    \langle O \rangle = \frac{\text{tr}\left(e^{-\beta \hat{K}} \hat{O}\right)}{\mathcal{Z}},
\]
where the grand partition function is defined as:
\[
    \mathcal{Z} = \text{tr}\left(e^{-\beta \hat{K}}\right).
\]
Here, \( \hat{K} = \hat{H} - \mu \hat{N} \) represents the grand canonical Hamiltonian. In the absolute zero temperature limit, \( T \to 0 \), the inverse temperature \( \beta \to +\infty \). In this asymptotic regime, the thermal average strictly reduces to a pure quantum mechanical expectation value over the ground state, where the grand-canonical ground state \( |\psi_0(\mu)\rangle \) is defined as the eigenstate of \( \hat{K} \) corresponding to its lowest possible eigenvalue\footnote{We are assuming that the ground state is unique. If this is not the case, a sum over all degenerate ground states would be required to evaluate the expectation values properly.}.

Based on our preceding diagonalization procedure, the lowest energy state corresponds to the configuration devoid of any quasiparticle excitations. Therefore, in the present case, \( |\psi_0(\mu)\rangle \) is precisely the vacuum of the new Bogoliubov operators \( \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} \), meaning that it must satisfy the annihilation condition:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bcs_vacuum_condition_gamma}
    \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} |\psi_0\rangle = 0 \quad \forall \mathbf{k}, \sigma.
\end{equation}
By explicitly using the Bogoliubov transformation derived earlier to express \( \gamma_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \) in terms of the bare particle operators, this vacuum condition for the specific spin-up branch (\( \sigma = \uparrow \)) yields:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bcs_vacuum_condition_bare}
    u_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} |\psi_0\rangle = v_{\mathbf{k}} c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger |\psi_0\rangle.
\end{equation}

Looking at Equation \ref{eq:bcs_vacuum_condition_bare}, we can observe a profound physical symmetry: the action of destroying a bare fermion with momentum \( \mathbf{k} \) and spin \( \uparrow \) on the ground state is directly proportional to creating a bare fermion with opposite momentum \( -\mathbf{k} \) and opposite spin \( \downarrow \). This entangled behavior is structurally very similar to what we previously found for the correlated ground state of a dilute interacting Bose gas. In that bosonic case, we determined that the ground state wave function was heavily populated by pairs of opposite momenta and could be expressed using an exponential ansatz:
\[
    |\psi_0\rangle = A e^{B a_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger a_{-\mathbf{k}}^\dagger} = A + \left(B a_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger a_{-\mathbf{k}}^\dagger\right) + \frac{A}{2} \left(B a_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger a_{-\mathbf{k}}^\dagger\right)^2 + \dots
\]
with \( A \) and \( B \) representing specific coherent coefficients that we determined.

However, in the present scenario, we are strictly dealing with fermions. Fermions are governed by the Pauli exclusion principle, which mathematically enforces that the square of any fermionic creation operator identically vanishes (\( c^\dagger c^\dagger = 0 \)). Consequently, all terms in the previous coherent expansion proportional to \( \left(a_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger a_{-\mathbf{k}}^\dagger\right)^n \) with \( n > 1 \) must vanish exactly for fermions.

Truncating the series to respect the Pauli principle, we thus try the following linear pairing ansatz for the wavefunction of a single momentum mode:
\[
    \left( A + B c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle,
\]
where \( |0\rangle \) represents the absolute vacuum of the original bare fermionic operators \( c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} \) (meaning a state containing zero particles). To determine the unknown coefficients \( A \) and \( B \), we strictly require that this proposed ansatz satisfies the defining vacuum condition \eqref{eq:bcs_vacuum_condition_bare}.

Evaluating the left-hand side of the condition by acting with the destruction operator yields:
\[
    u_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} \left( A + B c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle = B u_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger |0\rangle
\]
\[
    = B u_{\mathbf{k}} c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger |0\rangle.
\]
Evaluating the right-hand side of the condition by acting with the creation operator yields:
\[
    v_{\mathbf{k}} c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \left( A + B c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle = A v_{\mathbf{k}} c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger |0\rangle.
\]
By comparing these two results, we immediately see that the proposed state successfully verifies the vacuum condition if and only if \( B = v_{\mathbf{k}} \) and \( A = u_{\mathbf{k}} \). Because the different momentum modes \( \mathbf{k} \) are decoupled in the diagonalized Hamiltonian, this structural requirement must be independently satisfied for every momentum pair \( (\mathbf{k}\uparrow, -\mathbf{k}\downarrow) \) across the entire system. Taking the tensor product over all possible momentum states yields the complete many-body ground state:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bcs_wavefunction}
    |\psi_0(\mu)\rangle = \prod_{\mathbf{k}} \left( u_{\mathbf{k}} + v_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle.
\end{equation}

The state described by Equation \ref{eq:bcs_wavefunction} is the celebrated BCS wave function. It was first proposed historically by Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieffer in 1957 as a brilliant variational wave function to approximate the ground state of a metallic superconductor. The macroscopic state describes a coherent superposition where each momentum pair state \( (\mathbf{k}\uparrow, -\mathbf{k}\downarrow) \) is either empty with probability amplitude \( u_{\mathbf{k}} \), or occupied by a Cooper pair with probability amplitude \( v_{\mathbf{k}} \).

Finally, one can easily check that this constructed state is properly normalized to unity. Indeed, because different momentum modes commute, the norm factorizes as:
\[
    \langle \psi_0 | \psi_0 \rangle = \prod_{\mathbf{k}} \left( |u_{\mathbf{k}}|^2 + |v_{\mathbf{k}}|^2 \right) = 1,
\]
which is guaranteed to equal \( 1 \) thanks to the fundamental normalization condition on the coherence factors, \( |u_{\mathbf{k}}|^2 + |v_{\mathbf{k}}|^2 = 1 \), that we rigorously enforced during the Bogoliubov unitary transformation.
