\chapter[BCS Theory of Superconductivity]{BCS Theory of\\Superconductivity}

Superconductivity was experimentally observed well before the formal discovery of quantum mechanics, presenting a profound physical mystery that eventually necessitated the development of a comprehensive quantum many-body description. The definitive microscopic explanation of this phenomenon, known as the BCS theory, was developed in 1957 by John Bardeen, Leon Cooper, and John Robert Schrieffer. At its core, the theory explains the superconducting state through the formation of bound states of electrons, known as Cooper pairs.

To formally develop this, we will consider a general system composed of spin-1/2 fermions experiencing a mutual \emph{attractive} interaction between particles of opposite spin. Our primary theoretical interest is to determine the low-temperature phase of this many-body system. The central idea is that this mutual attraction can induce the formation of Cooper pairs—pairs of fermions with opposite spin. Because these composite pairs possess an integer total spin, they behave effectively as bosons and can undergo macroscopic condensation at sufficiently low temperatures.

The original motivation for studying such an interacting fermionic system was the microscopic explanation of metallic superconductivity. Superconductivity is a physical phenomenon occurring in certain metals and compounds whereby an electric current flows with absolutely zero electrical resistance below a specific critical temperature \( T_c \). This phenomenon is deeply analogous to superfluidity; whereas a superfluid involves the macroscopic flow of a fluid with zero viscosity (the mechanical analog of electrical resistance) below a critical temperature, a superconductor involves the frictionless flow of charged carriers, namely electrons.

A crucial thermodynamic clue pointing toward the existence of these bound pairs of fermions in a superconductor is provided by the electronic specific heat, \( c_v \). For an ideal Fermi gas at low temperatures \( T \)—which serves as a highly effective model for the conduction electron gas in a normal solid—the specific heat scales linearly with \( T \). However, when a material transitions into a superconductor, the specific heat is observed to vanish exponentially with decreasing temperature. This exponential suppression is the hallmark thermodynamic behavior expected when there is a finite energy gap in the excitation spectrum separating the macroscopic ground state from the first available excited states. The magnitude of this energy gap can be extracted directly from specific heat measurements, revealing it to be extremely small—typically several orders of magnitude smaller—compared to the Fermi energy \( E_F \) of the electrons. It is natural to guess that this energy gap is directly associated with the finite binding energy of the fermion pairs. (The microscopic mechanism by which normally repulsive electrons can experience an effective mutual attraction in a metal lattice will be discussed in detail in the subsequent section).

Beyond its foundational success in condensed matter systems, the BCS theoretical framework we are going to develop is remarkably universal. The collection of phenomena that can be governed by these same equations spans an astonishing range of more than ten orders of magnitude in temperature. At the lower extreme, in the nanokelvin regime, the theory applies directly to ultracold Fermi gases trapped in optical lattices. These are dilute, neutral systems of fermionic atoms where an effective mutual attraction—arising fundamentally from van der Waals forces—can be accurately modeled as a simple contact interaction due to the extreme diluteness of the gas. These highly tunable systems have been studied very actively over recent years because experimentalists can artificially tune the strength of the interparticle interaction at will. The theory is equally relevant to other macroscopic neutral systems, such as liquid \( ^3\text{He} \), where fermionic neutral atoms also experience a mutual attraction. At the absolute opposite extreme of the temperature and density scale, the very same BCS formalism can be extended to describe the pairing of quarks inside the ultra-dense, extremely high-temperature cores of neutron stars (a phenomenon known as color superconductivity).

\section{Basic Model for Effective Attraction}

Let us consider a simplified model of a solid consisting of a cubic lattice of positively charged ions with a lattice spacing \( a \), permeated by a gas of itinerant electrons. We assume the system is isolated, without any external electric or magnetic fields. The electrons are subject to a periodic potential generated by the ionic lattice; consequently, the potential minima are located in the immediate vicinity of the positive ions, marking the regions where the electrons are most likely to be found.

The electrons interact not only with this periodic ionic potential but also with one another via the standard Coulomb repulsion, \( V_{e-e} \sim e^2/r \). However, the presence of the mobile electron gas embedded in the positively charged background activates the many-body phenomenon of screening. In a metal, the spatial redistribution of conduction electrons around any single negative charge effectively screens that negative charge at a distance from it. This screening effect drastically modifies the long-range Coulomb repulsion into a short-range effective interaction, which decays exponentially as \( \sim e^{-r/\xi} \), where the screening length \( \xi \) is typically on the order of a few lattice spacings \( a \). Although this screened interaction is significantly weaker at macroscopic distances, it remains fundamentally repulsive. To explain the formation of Cooper pairs, we must identify a physical mechanism capable of overcoming this residual repulsion to produce a net effective attraction between the electrons.

\subsubsection{The Electron-Phonon Interaction and Fröhlich Mechanism}

This requisite attraction arises from the interaction between the electrons and the collective vibrations of the ionic lattice, known as phonons. Because the typical Debye temperature characterizing these macroscopic phonon modes is on the order of \( \omega_D \sim 100\,\text{K} \), while superconducting phenomena typically occur at cryogenic temperatures around \( 1\,\text{K} \), the thermal phonons are effectively frozen out. Consequently, we can safely treat the quantized phonon field as a perturbation to the interacting electron system.

As famously demonstrated by H. Fröhlich, in certain metals, the combination of the screening of the Coulomb repulsion and the virtual exchange of phonons between electrons may lead to an overall effective attraction between them. Specifically, the dynamic interaction between electrons and phonons produces a retarded attractive force. When an electron moves through the crystal, it locally modifies the ionic lattice by pulling the positive ions slightly toward its trajectory, creating a localized cloud of positive charges, and then rapidly moves away. Subsequently, a second electron may be electrostatically attracted by this localized accumulation of positive charge.

A key physical ingredient of this retarded mechanism is the stark difference in dynamical time scales: the heavy distortions of the ionic lattice fade away rather slowly in comparison with the extremely fast time scale over which one electron is replaced by a second one at a certain position due to the rapid motion of the electrons. Ultimately, these two combined effects—electronic screening and virtual phonon exchange—may lead in certain materials to an overall effective attraction between the electrons, provided those electrons belong to a tiny energy shell situated immediately around the Fermi energy \( E_F \).


\subsection{Effective Interaction}

We aim to describe the behavior of electrons in a solid and understand how they interact with the lattice vibrations. A solid can be modeled as a static lattice of ions, with electrons occupying localized orbitals and tunneling through them. The classical positions of the ions are perturbed by dynamical excitations, which we describe as \textbf{phonons}.

In the harmonic approximation, the phonon Hamiltonian is constructed as a sum over independent harmonic oscillators:
\[
    H_{\text{ion}} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\mathbf{q}} \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}} a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger a_{\mathbf{q}},
\]
where \(a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger\) and \(a_{\mathbf{q}}\) are the bosonic creation and annihilation operators for a phonon with momentum \(\mathbf{q}\), and \(\omega_{\mathbf{q}}\) is the phonon frequency.

The Hamiltonian for the electrons is composed of an interaction part and a kinetic part:
\[
    H_{\text{el}} = H_{\text{int}} + H_{\text{kin}}.
\]

\paragraph{Interaction Hamiltonian.} Naively, the interaction between electrons is the bare Coulomb repulsion. However, the presence of a background of positive charges screens this interaction. The effective screened potential is often described by the Thomas-Fermi model, which introduces an exponential decay over the distance \(|\mathbf{r}|\):
\[
    V(\mathbf{r}) \propto \frac{e^{-|\mathbf{r}|/\xi}}{|\mathbf{r}|} \sim e^{-|\mathbf{r}|/\xi},
\]
where \(\xi\) is the Thomas-Fermi screening length.

While the kinetic energy can be formulated on a real-space lattice (yielding the Hubbard model), it is much more convenient to diagonalize it directly in momentum space. Assuming that the kinetic energy is independent of the electron spin \(\sigma\), we have:
\[
    H_{\text{kin}} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma},
\]
where \(c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}^\dagger\) and \(c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}\) are the fermionic creation and annihilation operators for an electron with momentum \(\mathbf{k}\) and spin \(\sigma\), and \(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m}\) is the bare electron dispersion.

\paragraph{Total Hamiltonian.} Combining the lattice and electronic components, the total Hamiltonian is:
\[
    H = H_{\text{ion}} + H_{\text{int}} + H_{\text{kin}}.
\]
Our ultimate goal is to use perturbation theory to trace out the phonon degrees of freedom. This procedure will yield an effective Hamiltonian that only contains the fermionic operators \((c, c^\dagger)\), effectively incorporating the phonons as a mediated interaction between the electrons:
\[
    H \to H_{\text{eff}}(c, c^\dagger).
\]

\subsection{Single Particle Problem}

\paragraph{Electron-Phonon Interaction.} How can an electron interact with a phonon? By traveling through the solid, an electron can kick the ions and excite their physical modes. Based on the principle of Occam's razor, we consider only the simplest fundamental processes: the emission and the absorption of a single phonon.

The phenomenological interaction potential \(V_1\) describing these scattering processes must contain a sum over all possible electron momenta \(\mathbf{k}\), spins \(\sigma\), and phonon momenta \(\mathbf{q}\):
\[
    V_1 = \sum_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} \sum_{\mathbf{q}} \left( M_{\mathbf{q}, \sigma} a_{\mathbf{q}} c_{\mathbf{k} + \mathbf{q}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} + M_{-\mathbf{q}, \sigma} a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{q}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} \right).
\]
The amplitude of this interaction is governed by the matrix element \(M_{\mathbf{q}, \sigma}\), which takes the form:
\[
    M_{\mathbf{q}, \sigma} \simeq \frac{|\mathbf{q}|}{\sqrt{M_{\text{ion}} \omega_{\mathbf{q}}}}.
\]
Physically, \(M_{\mathbf{q}, \sigma}\) is inversely proportional to the square root of the ion mass \(M_{\text{ion}}\). If the lattice is composed of heavy ions, it is extremely challenging for the electrons to excite the phononic modes, causing the matrix element to vanish rapidly. Conversely, lighter ions allow for an easier transfer of energy from the electrons to the ion chain.

\paragraph{Perturbation Theory in $V_1$.} Our full system is described by:
\[
    H = H_{\text{kin}} + H_{\text{ph}} + V_1.
\]
We proceed by treating \(V_1\) as a perturbation. To do so, we must first define the unperturbed vacuum (ground state) for both fermions and bosons.

\paragraph{Unperturbed Vacuum.} We evaluate the vacuum in the presence of some initial particle excitations. This state, \(|\phi_{n_e, n_{ph}}\rangle\), is formed by the tensor product of the electron and phonon states:
\[
    |\phi_{n_e, n_{ph}}\rangle = \left( \prod_{\substack{\mathbf{k}_j = \mathbf{k}_1, \dots, \mathbf{k}_{n_e} \\ \sigma_j = \sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_{n_e}}} c_{\mathbf{k}_j, \sigma_j}^\dagger \right) \left( \prod_{\mathbf{q}_j = \mathbf{q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{q}_{n_{ph}}} a_{\mathbf{q}_j}^\dagger \right) |0, 0\rangle.
\]
Here, the absolute vacuum is defined as \(|0, 0\rangle = |n_e = 0, n_{ph} = 0\rangle\). The phonon vacuum itself is a tensor product over all possible momentum states:
\[
    |0, 0\rangle = |0\rangle_{\mathbf{q}_1} \otimes |0\rangle_{\mathbf{q}_2} \otimes \dots
\]
Because the system is confined in a box/lattice, the allowed momenta are discrete and defined within the Brillouin zone. For example, along the \(x\)-direction:
\[
    q_x^{(J)} = \frac{2\pi J}{L_x}, \quad J \in \mathbb{Z}.
\]
For the electrons, this highly populated unperturbed ground state is commonly referred to as the \textbf{Fermi Sea}.

\subsubsection{First Order Perturbation Theory}

Let us compute the first-order correction to the energy, which fundamentally requires evaluating the expectation value of the perturbation \(V_1\) over the unperturbed vacuum state \(|\phi_0\rangle\):
\[
    E_1 = \langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle.
\]
If we define our initial state explicitly by its definite number of electrons and phonons, \(|\phi_0\rangle = |n_e, n_{ph}\rangle\), applying the interaction potential \(V_1\) physically yields a new state where the total number of phonons has changed by exactly one (due to the fundamental processes of either a single emission or a single absorption):
\[
    V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle = |n_e, n_{ph} \pm 1\rangle.
\]
Because the number of phonons strictly changes under the action of \(V_1\), the resulting state is mathematically orthogonal to the initial state. Under the physically motivated assumption that the phonons do not macroscopically condense (which would give them a finite vacuum expectation value), the first-order energy correction strictly vanishes:
\[
    \implies \langle n_e, n_{ph} | n_e, n_{ph} \pm 1 \rangle = 0.
\]

\subsubsection{Second Order Perturbation Theory}

Since the leading first-order term is identically zero, we must evaluate the perturbation to second order to capture the leading interaction effects. The second-order energy correction \(E_2\) is formally given by summing over all possible intermediate states \(|\phi_{n,m}\rangle\):
\[
    E_2 = \sum_{n,m} \frac{\langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \phi_{n,m} \rangle \langle \phi_{n,m} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle}{E_0^{(\text{unp.})} - E_{n,m}^{(\text{unp.})}}.
\]
Out of this seemingly infinite sum over all basis states, only highly specific terms survive the projection. The number of electrons \(n\) in the intermediate state must strictly remain equal to \(n_e\), and the number of phonons \(m\) must be exactly \(n_{ph} + 1\) or \(n_{ph} - 1\). This selection rule allows us to simplify the numerator structure using Kronecker deltas:
\[
    E_2 = \sum_{n,m} \frac{\delta_{n,n_e} \left( \delta_{m,n_{ph}+1} + \delta_{m,n_{ph}-1} \right)}{E_0^{(\text{unp.})} - E_{n,m}^{(\text{unp.})}}.
\]

\begin{example}[Single electron application]
    To intimately understand the underlying physics, let us perform the explicit calculation for the simplest possible case: a single electron with initial momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) and spin \(\sigma\), and exactly zero initial phonons (\(n_e = 1\), \(n_{ph} = 0\)). The initial state is constructed as \(|\phi_0\rangle = c_{\mathbf{p},\sigma}^\dagger |0\rangle\). Applying the interaction \(V_1\) to this specific state and carefully enforcing both momentum and spin conservation through the resulting delta functions, we obtain:
    \[
        V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle = \sum_{\mathbf{q},\sigma'} \sum_{\mathbf{k}'} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{-\mathbf{q}}) c_{\mathbf{k}'+\mathbf{q},\sigma'}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}',\sigma'} c_{\mathbf{p},\sigma}^\dagger |0\rangle
    \]
    \[
        = \sum_{\mathbf{q},\sigma'} \sum_{\mathbf{k}'} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{-\mathbf{q}}) c_{\mathbf{k}'+\mathbf{q},\sigma'}^\dagger \delta_{\sigma,\sigma'} \delta_{\mathbf{k}',\mathbf{p}} |0\rangle
    \]
    \[
        = \sum_{\mathbf{q}} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{-\mathbf{q}}) c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\sigma}^\dagger |0\rangle.
    \]
    This rigorous evaluation explicitly shows the initial electron's momentum being shifted by \(\mathbf{q}\) due to the interaction with the phonon field.

    Now, we compute the quantum overlap of this resulting state with a generic intermediate state \(|\phi_{n,m}\rangle\). For a non-zero overlap to exist, the intermediate state must contain exactly 1 electron and 1 phonon (\(n = 1\), \(m = 1\)), meaning its bra form is constructed as \(\langle \phi_{n,m} | = \langle 0 | c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}',\sigma'} a_{\mathbf{q}'}\). Taking the inner product systematically isolates the exact phonon mode that was emitted:
    \[
        \langle \phi_{n,m} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle = \left( \sum_{\mathbf{q}',\sigma'} \langle 0 | c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}',\sigma'} a_{\mathbf{q}'} \right) \left( \sum_{\mathbf{q}} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{-\mathbf{q}}) c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\sigma}^\dagger |0\rangle \right)
    \]
    \[
        = \sum_{\sigma',\mathbf{q}'} \delta_{\sigma,\sigma'} \delta_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}'} M_{\mathbf{q}} = M_{\mathbf{q}}.
    \]

    Finally, substituting this squared matrix element back into the formal second-order energy formula, we must evaluate the corresponding energy denominator. The initial unperturbed energy is simply the kinetic energy of the single electron, \(E_0^{(\text{unp.})} = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}}\), and the intermediate unperturbed energy includes both the shifted electron and the newly emitted phonon, \(E_{n,m}^{(\text{unp.})} = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} + \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}\). Thus, the second-order correction is:
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:single_electron_shift}
        E_2 = \frac{M_{\mathbf{q}}^2}{-\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}} + (\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}})}.
    \end{equation}
    This crucial formula explicitly defines the effective energy shift that the virtual phonon cloud induces on a single isolated fermion.
\end{example}

\subsection{Two Body Problem}

To rigorously study the effective electron-electron interactions mediated by phonons, we must transition from a single-particle picture to an initial unperturbed state \( |\phi_0\rangle \) containing at least two particles. We define this two-fermion ground state as:
\[
    |\phi_0\rangle = c_{\mathbf{p},\alpha}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}',\alpha'}^\dagger |0\rangle.
\]

As established previously, the first-order perturbation theory correction trivially vanishes because the interaction Hamiltonian strictly creates or destroys a single phonon, ensuring orthogonality with the initial phonon-free state. We therefore proceed directly to evaluate the second-order energy correction, which requires computing the transition matrix elements connecting our initial two-particle state to the virtual intermediate states:
\[
    \langle \phi_{n,m} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle \quad \text{and} \quad \langle \phi_{2,1} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle.
\]
Applying the electron-phonon interaction operator \( V_1 \) to our initial state allows the fermionic destruction operator \( c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} \) within \( V_1 \) to contract with either of the two initial electrons (possessing momenta \( \mathbf{p} \) or \( \mathbf{p}' \)):
\[
    V_1 |\phi_0\rangle = \sum_{\mathbf{q}} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{-\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{\mathbf{q}}) \sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} c_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma} c_{\mathbf{p},\alpha}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}',\alpha'}^\dagger |0\rangle
\]
\[
    = \sum_{\mathbf{q}} M_{\mathbf{q}} (a_{-\mathbf{q}}^\dagger + a_{\mathbf{q}}) \left( c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\alpha}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}',\alpha'}^\dagger + c_{\mathbf{p},\alpha}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}'+\mathbf{q},\alpha'}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle.
\]

These two diagrams correspond precisely to the two possible intermediate states containing exactly one virtual phonon, which can be emitted by either the first electron or the second electron. The bra forms of these specific intermediate states that yield a non-zero overlap are given by:
\[
    \langle \phi_{2,1} | = \langle 0 | a_{-\mathbf{q}} c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\alpha} c_{\mathbf{p}',\alpha'},
\]
\[
    \langle \tilde{\phi}_{2,1} | = \langle 0 | a_{\mathbf{q}} c_{\mathbf{p},\alpha} c_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q},\alpha'}.
\]

We now substitute these transition matrix elements into the standard second-order perturbation formula. The expectation values of the paired creation and annihilation operators simply trace out to unity, allowing us to factor out the squared electron-phonon matrix element \( M_{\mathbf{q}}^2 \):
\[
    E_2' = \left[ \frac{\langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \phi_{2,1} \rangle \langle \phi_{2,1} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle}{E_0 - E_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})}} + \frac{\langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \tilde{\phi}_{2,1} \rangle \langle \tilde{\phi}_{2,1} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle}{E_0 - \tilde{E}_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})}} \right]
\]
\[
    = M_{\mathbf{q}}^2 \left( \frac{1}{E_0 - E_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})}} + \frac{1}{E_0 - \tilde{E}_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})}} \right).
\]
By evaluating this algebraic sum, we can determine whether the resulting energy shift represents a repulsive or an attractive effective interaction between the electrons, a property completely dictated by the sign of the unperturbed energy denominators.

Defining the initial unperturbed energy and the intermediate unperturbed energies for these two distinct exchange processes, we have:
\[
    E_0 = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} + \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'},
\]
\[
    E_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})} = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} + \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'} + \hbar\omega_{-\mathbf{q}},
\]
\[
    \tilde{E}_{2,1}^{(\text{unp.})} = \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q}} + \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} + \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}.
\]
Substituting these explicit energies into our second-order expression, the energy shift \( E_2' \) becomes:
\[
    E_2' = M_{\mathbf{q}}^2 \left( \frac{1}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} - \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}} + \frac{1}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q}} - \hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}} \right).
\]
To simplify this result, we apply the physical limit of a small momentum exchange \( \mathbf{q} \). We assume the relevant scattering processes keep the electrons tightly confined close to the Fermi surface (within the heavily populated Fermi sea), which imposes the approximate kinematic relation:
\[
    \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} \simeq \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}'}.
\]
Applying this small-\( \mathbf{q} \) approximation, the effective energy shift neatly reduces to the Fröhlich interaction form:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:frohlich_effective_interaction}
    E_2' \simeq M_{\mathbf{q}}^2 \left( \frac{2\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}}}{(\epsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{p}})^2 - (\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}})^2} \right).
\end{equation}
This pivotal result reveals that the effective phonon-mediated interaction can dynamically manifest as either attractive or repulsive, depending entirely on the sign of the energy denominator.

\subsubsection{Attractive Interaction and Isotope Effect}

We now restrict our focus to the low-temperature regime, specifically when \( T \ll T_D \), where \( T_D \sim \omega_D \) is the characteristic Debye temperature of the crystal lattice. In this regime, the electrons lack the thermal energy necessary to scatter far from the Fermi surface. Consequently, the kinetic energy difference between the initial and scattered electron states is constrained to be much smaller than the typical phonon energy (the Debye energy):
\[
    |\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}} - \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}| \ll \hbar\omega_D.
\]
Under this stringent condition, the denominator in Equation \ref{eq:frohlich_effective_interaction} is overwhelmingly dominated by the phonon energy squared term, \( -(\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}})^2 \), ensuring it naturally becomes negative. The effective interaction therefore simplifies to a constant, weakly attractive potential:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:attractive_potential_approx}
    E_2' \simeq \frac{-|M_{\mathbf{q}}|^2}{\hbar\omega_D}.
\end{equation}

Recall from our fundamental coupling definitions that the squared electron-phonon matrix element, \( |M_{\mathbf{q}}|^2 \), is inversely proportional to the mass of the lattice ions (\( M_{\text{ion}} \)). Therefore, the overall strength of the attractive interaction scales directly as:
\[
    E_2' \propto \frac{1}{M_{\text{ion}}}.
\]
This simple inverse proportionality provides a profound microscopic explanation for the experimentally observed \textbf{isotope effect} in conventional superconductors, where the critical transition temperature is found to vary predictably with the specific mass of the isotopes that comprise the crystal lattice.

\begin{remark}
    While our derivation treats \( V_1 \) as a small perturbation within a simplified cubic lattice, this is not always universally applicable. Because the matrix element \( M_{\mathbf{q}} \) scales inversely with the ion mass, the effective electron-phonon interaction can become exceedingly strong in materials composed of very light ions. In such strong-coupling regimes, the perturbative approach breaks down, necessitating more advanced non-perturbative methods (such as Eliashberg theory). Furthermore, real materials often possess complex crystal geometries far beyond a simple cubic structure; these specific geometric details fundamentally alter the phonon spectrum and, consequently, the precise structure of the effective electron-electron interaction.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{The BCS Effective Potential}

For the specific purposes of successfully developing the macroscopic BCS theory (Step 1 of the paradigm), we treat this phonon-mediated interaction as a separable, strictly attractive potential. We completely drop its explicit momentum dependence in favor of a constant effective coupling strength, phenomenologically denoted as \( -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \).

Furthermore, while the complete interaction Hamiltonian realistically includes the residual screened Coulomb repulsion (the Thomas-Fermi term), the fundamental assumption of BCS theory is that the attractive phonon-mediated part robustly dominates for electrons near the Fermi surface. Thus, we neglect the residual electrostatic repulsion and approximate the total effective electron-electron potential as purely attractive:
\[
    V_{e-e,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}'} \simeq -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 + \cancel{V_{\text{Thomas-Fermi}}} \simeq -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2.
\]

It is crucial to emphasize that while this attractive energy shift \( \Delta E = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \) (derived by discarding scattering processes with energy exchanges far beyond the Debye cutoff \( \omega_D \)) is exceedingly small, it is fundamentally sufficient to destabilize the unperturbed Fermi sea and induce the formation of a bound state between two electrons. Having established the scalar amplitude of this attractive potential, the immediate next step is to rigorously construct the full operatorial part of the interaction Hamiltonian to be utilized in solving the macroscopic many-body problem.





We are assuming weak interactions, but that is not always the case: the coefficient \(M(\mathbf{q})\) is inversely proportional to the mass of the ions in the lattice, and thus the interaction can be very strong in the case of light ions. In this case, we need to use a non-perturbative approach.

One has to pay attention also to the geometry of the lattice, since our treatment was based on a cubic lattice, but in general the lattice can be more complicated. The geometry of the lattice can affect the phonon spectrum and thus the effective interaction between electrons.

We got \(\Delta E = - \vert g_{eff} \vert^2 \) for phonons with \(\omega < \omega_D\) (discarding processes going to far away from the Fermi surface). This is a very small energy, but it is enough to create a bound state of two electrons. Now we have to find the operatorial part of the Hamiltonian, which is the part that will be used in the many-body problem.

\subsection{Effective Hamiltonian and Two-Body Ground State}

Our primary objective is to trace out the phononic degrees of freedom completely and construct an effective electronic Hamiltonian \( H^{(2)} \) valid up to second order in perturbation theory:
\[
    H^{(2)} = H_0 + H_2.
\]
Rigorously, this systematic decoupling of the bosonic modes is achieved through a canonical \textbf{Schrieffer-Wolff transformation}. However, we can intuitively reconstruct the operatorial form of the effective interaction \( H_2 \) (often denoted as \( H_{\text{int}} \)) directly from the second-order transition matrix elements evaluated previously:
\[
    \langle \phi_0 | V_1 | \phi_{mn} \rangle \langle \phi_{mn} | V_1 | \phi_0 \rangle.
\]
Physically, this second-order process corresponds to two incoming fermions exchanging a virtual phonon of momentum \( \mathbf{q} \), as depicted in the following Feynman diagram:

In the second quantization formalism, the general interacting part of the many-body Hamiltonian, \( H_{\text{int}} \), capturing this fundamental scattering process between pairs of fermions (parameterized in terms of center-of-mass and relative momenta), can be modeled as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bcs_interaction_hamiltonian}
    H_{\text{int}} = \frac{1}{2V} \sum_{\mathbf{k}, \mathbf{k}', \mathbf{q}} \sum_{\sigma, \sigma'} V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) c_{\mathbf{k}' + \frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}' + \frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}, \sigma'}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k} + \frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}, \sigma'} c_{\mathbf{k} + \frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}, \sigma},
\end{equation}
where \( V \) is the macroscopic volume of the system, and \( c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma} \) and \( c_{\mathbf{k}, \sigma}^\dagger \) are respectively the fermionic annihilation and creation operators for an electron possessing momentum \( \mathbf{k} \) and spin projection \( \sigma \). The scattering amplitude is governed by the matrix element \( V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) \). We sum over all possible incoming and outgoing relative momenta (as well as over all feasible values of the center-of-mass momentum \( \mathbf{q} \)), and over the spin indices \( \sigma \) and \( \sigma' \). The prefactor of \( 1/2 \) is explicitly included to avoid double counting of identical two-body scattering processes.

Translating our specific phonon-exchange diagram into second quantization operators using the constant attractive BCS coupling \( -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \), the effective two-body interaction term \( H_2 \) explicitly takes the form:
\[
    H_2 = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \sum_{\substack{\sigma, \sigma' \\ \mathbf{p}, \mathbf{p}' \\ \mathbf{q} \in \text{shell}}} \left[ \left( c_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}'-\mathbf{q}, \sigma'}^\dagger \right) \left( c_{\mathbf{p}', \sigma'} c_{\mathbf{p}, \sigma} \right) + \left( c_{\mathbf{p}, \sigma}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{p}', \sigma'}^\dagger \right) \left( c_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}, \sigma} c_{\mathbf{p}'+\mathbf{q}, \sigma'} \right) \right].
\]
The summation constraint \( \mathbf{q} \in \text{shell} \) mathematically enforces the crucial physical requirement that the attractive interaction only operates for small energy exchanges bounded by the Debye frequency \( \omega_D \).

Notice how the phonon operators have completely disappeared from the final expression for the interaction Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:bcs_interaction_hamiltonian}. By casting the Hamiltonian in this form, we have successfully integrated out the bosonic degrees of freedom, reducing the complex coupled electron-phonon problem to a purely fermionic model governed by an effective attractive potential. The phonons are still physically present in the crystal, but they are now treated purely as virtual excitations that mediate the interaction between the electrons.

Our goal is now to solve the many-body problem of interacting fermions governed by \( H = H_0 + H_2 \), which remains a highly non-trivial task. Thus, we will initially limit our analysis to the simplest possible model that still captures the essential physics of the instability: finding the ground state of a system containing just two electrons interacting via the effective potential \( V(\mathbf{k}', \mathbf{k}) \) on top of an inert, filled Fermi sea of non-interacting electrons (which blocks states below \( k_F \) via Pauli exclusion). This is the celebrated \textbf{Cooper problem}, and it serves as the foundational starting point for the full macroscopic BCS theory.

\subsubsection{Solving the Two-Electron Ground State: Wavefunction Factorization}

To solve the effective Hamiltonian \( H = H_0 + H_2 \) for the two-electron ground state, we define the general two-particle wavefunction as a function of both the spatial positions and spin projections of the electrons:
\[
    \Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \sigma_1; \mathbf{r}_2, \sigma_2).
\]
Assuming spatial translational invariance across the crystal, we can cleanly separate the center-of-mass (CM) motion from the relative motion. The center-of-mass motion is described by a simple plane wave \( e^{i\mathbf{K}_{\text{CM}} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{\text{CM}}} \). Furthermore, because our starting Hamiltonian does not contain terms that explicitly couple spin and momentum (i.e., there is no entanglement between the external positional degrees of freedom and the internal spin degrees of freedom), we can factorize the wavefunction into a spatial part and a spin part:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cooper_wavefunction_ansatz}
    \Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \sigma_1; \mathbf{r}_2, \sigma_2) = e^{i\mathbf{K}_{\text{CM}} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{\text{CM}}} \varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) \phi^{\text{spin}}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2),
\end{equation}
where \( \varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) \) is the external spatial wavefunction governing the relative motion, and \( \phi^{\text{spin}}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) \) is the internal spin wavefunction. (Note that to maximize the phase space available for scattering near the Fermi surface and achieve the lowest possible ground state energy, one typically sets the center-of-mass momentum to zero, \( \mathbf{K}_{\text{CM}} = 0 \)).

\begin{remark}
    The clean factorization of the wavefunction into spatial and spin components is strictly valid because the Hamiltonian can be separated into operators acting solely on position and spin, respectively: \( H = \hat{\Lambda}_1^{\mathbf{r}} \hat{\Lambda}_2^{\sigma} \). This separability is a general feature of models that neglect relativistic spin-orbit coupling. A prominent example where local interactions are treated explicitly alongside spin is the \textbf{Hubbard Model}:
    \[
        H_{\text{Hubbard}} = U n_{i\uparrow} n_{i\downarrow},
    \]
    where \( U > 0 \) represents an on-site Coulomb repulsion energy penalty, which is fundamental for studying strongly correlated phenomena such as the \textbf{Mott Transition}. However, in our current derivation for conventional superconductivity, we utilize the phonon-mediated attractive potential \( H_2 \) and assume spin-orbit effects are negligible.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Spin Wavefunction and Fermionic Antisymmetry}

For a system composed of two spin-\( 1/2 \) particles, the composite spin state can be configured either as a symmetric triplet (\( S=1 \)) or an antisymmetric singlet (\( S=0 \)):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Triplet (\( S=1 \), symmetric):} \( |\uparrow_1 \uparrow_2\rangle, \quad |\downarrow_1 \downarrow_2\rangle, \quad \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow_1 \downarrow_2\rangle + |\downarrow_1 \uparrow_2\rangle) \).
    \item \textbf{Singlet (\( S=0 \), antisymmetric):} \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow_1 \downarrow_2\rangle - |\downarrow_1 \uparrow_2\rangle) \).
\end{itemize}
The triplet states are energetically unfavorable in this simplified model. To maximally benefit from the localized attractive potential mediated by the phonons, the two electrons must have a finite probability of occupying the exact same local spatial site (i.e., \( \mathbf{r}_1 = \mathbf{r}_2 \)). The Pauli exclusion principle strictly forbids two electrons with parallel spins (triplets) from occupying the same spatial position, forcing their spatial wavefunction to vanish at zero separation. Similarly, a simple magnetic interaction model \( H \propto \mathbf{S}_1 \cdot \mathbf{S}_2 \) naturally selects the singlet as the lowest energy ground state. Therefore, we assume the Cooper pair forms in a spin singlet state:
\[
    \phi^{\text{spin}}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( |\uparrow_1 \downarrow_2\rangle - |\downarrow_1 \uparrow_2\rangle \right).
\]

Because electrons are fundamental fermions, the total many-body wavefunction must be strictly antisymmetric under the simultaneous exchange of both particles' spatial and spin coordinates:
\[
    \Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \sigma_1; \mathbf{r}_2, \sigma_2) = -\Psi(\mathbf{r}_2, \sigma_2; \mathbf{r}_1, \sigma_1).
\]
Substituting our factorized ansatz \eqref{eq:cooper_wavefunction_ansatz} into this condition (and noting that the center-of-mass coordinate \( \mathbf{R}_{\text{CM}} = (\mathbf{r}_1 + \mathbf{r}_2)/2 \) remains invariant upon swapping particles 1 and 2), we obtain:
\[
    \varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) \phi^{\text{spin}}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = -\varphi(\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1) \phi^{\text{spin}}(\sigma_2, \sigma_1).
\]
Since we established that the spin component is a singlet, it is explicitly antisymmetric under spin exchange:
\[
    \phi^{\text{spin}}(\sigma_2, \sigma_1) = -\phi^{\text{spin}}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2).
\]
The negative signs cancel out on the right-hand side, which imposes a strict parity symmetry condition on the spatial part of the wavefunction:
\[
    \varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) = +\varphi(\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1).
\]
Defining the relative distance vector as \( \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2 \), this simplifies to:
\[
    \varphi(\mathbf{r}) = +\varphi(-\mathbf{r}).
\]
We conclude that for a spin-singlet pair, the relative spatial wavefunction \( \varphi(\mathbf{r}) \) must be an \textbf{even function} of the relative separation (corresponding to \( s \)-wave, \( d \)-wave, etc., orbital symmetries).

\subsubsection{Fourier Expansion and the Eigenvalue Equation}

Our goal is now to determine the explicit functional form of \( \varphi(\mathbf{r}) \). Because the problem depends solely on the relative distance, it is vastly simpler to treat in momentum space. We expand the relative spatial wavefunction into its Fourier modes \( \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} \):
\[
    \varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)}.
\]
Applying the spatial even-parity symmetry condition \( \varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) = +\varphi(\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1) \), we can manipulate the expansion by relabeling the dummy summation index \( \mathbf{k} \to -\mathbf{k} \):
\[
    \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} = \varphi(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) = +\varphi(\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{-i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} = \sum_{-\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{-\mathbf{k}} e^{+i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{-\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)}.
\]
Comparing the first and last expressions, since the sum over all \( \mathbf{k} \) and \( -\mathbf{k} \) spans the exact same momentum space, the Fourier coefficients themselves must be symmetric:
\[
    \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} = \varphi_{-\mathbf{k}}.
\]

We now wish to solve the time-independent Schrödinger eigenvalue equation for the two-body ground state energy \( E \) in the center-of-mass frame (\( \mathbf{K}_{\text{CM}} = 0 \)):
\[
    E \Psi = H \Psi = (H_0 + H_{\text{int}}) \Psi, \quad \text{with} \quad \Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \sigma_1; \mathbf{r}_2, \sigma_2) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \phi_{S=0}^{\text{spin}}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2).
\]
To find the closed equation governing the coefficients \( \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} \), we transition fully to the second quantization representation and project the Schrödinger equation onto a specific basis state \( \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | \):
\[
    \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | E | \Psi \rangle = \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | H_0 + H_{\text{int}} | \Psi \rangle,
\]
where the total two-particle state is expanded as \( |\Psi\rangle = \sum_{\mathbf{k}'}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}'} |\Psi_{\mathbf{k}'}\rangle \) (the prime on the summation indicates that the momenta are restricted to the narrow attractive energy shell above the filled Fermi sea), and the normalized basis state for a spin-singlet pair with opposite momenta \( (\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k}) \) is defined in second quantization as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:singlet_basis_state}
    |\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( c_{\mathbf{k}, \uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}, \downarrow}^\dagger - c_{-\mathbf{k}, \uparrow}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}, \downarrow}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle.
\end{equation}

We now split the evaluation of \( H |\Psi\rangle \) into two parts: the free non-interacting kinetic part and the interaction part. The action of the kinetic Hamiltonian \( H_0 \) is straightforward:
\[
    H_0 |\Psi\rangle = \left( \frac{\hat{p}_1^2}{2m} + \frac{\hat{p}_2^2}{2m} \right) \left[ \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \phi_{S=0}^{\text{spin}} \right] = (\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} + \epsilon_{-\mathbf{k}}) \left[ \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \phi_{S=0}^{\text{spin}} \right] = 2 \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} |\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle,
\]
where we used the isotropic dispersion symmetry \( \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} = \epsilon_{-\mathbf{k}} = \hbar^2 k^2 / (2m) \).

Next, we evaluate the action of the attractive interaction term \( H_{\text{int}} \) (or \( H_2 \)) on the state \( |\Psi\rangle \):
\[
    H_{\text{int}} |\Psi\rangle = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \sum_{\sigma_1, \sigma_2} \sum_{\mathbf{k}_1, \mathbf{k}_2} \sum_{\mathbf{q}}' c_{\mathbf{k}_1 + \mathbf{q}, \sigma_1}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}_2 - \mathbf{q}, \sigma_2}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}_1, \sigma_1} c_{\mathbf{k}_2, \sigma_2} \left( \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \right) \left( c_{\mathbf{k}, \uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}, \downarrow}^\dagger - c_{-\mathbf{k}, \uparrow}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}, \downarrow}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle.
\]
Contracting the annihilation operators \( c_{\mathbf{k}_1, \sigma_1} c_{\mathbf{k}_2, \sigma_2} \) with the creation operators of the singlet state resolves the internal summations over \( \mathbf{k}_1, \mathbf{k}_2 \) and spins (specifically selecting the anti-parallel configurations such as \( \sigma_1 = \uparrow, \sigma_2 = \downarrow \)) via Kronecker deltas. After performing this operator algebra, the scattered state simplifies to:
\[
    H_{\text{int}} |\Psi\rangle = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \sum_{\mathbf{q}}' \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \left( c_{\mathbf{k} + \mathbf{q}, \uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{q}, \downarrow}^\dagger - c_{-\mathbf{k} + \mathbf{q}, \uparrow}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{q}, \downarrow}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle.
\]

Now we compute the complete matrix elements by projecting onto the bra \( \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | \). For the left-hand side of the Schrödinger equation, the orthogonality of the basis states immediately yields:
\[
    \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | E | \Psi \rangle = E \varphi_{\mathbf{k}}.
\]
For the right-hand side, we bracket the kinetic and interaction results with \( \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | \):
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | H_0 + H_{\text{int}} | \Psi \rangle & = 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} + \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | H_{\text{int}} | \Psi \rangle                                                                                                                                                                              \\
                                                                        & = 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} + \langle 0 | \left( c_{-\mathbf{k}, \downarrow} c_{\mathbf{k}, \uparrow} - c_{\mathbf{k}, \downarrow} c_{-\mathbf{k}, \uparrow} \right) \sum_{\mathbf{k}'}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}'} e^{-i\mathbf{k}' \cdot (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)} \\
                                                                        & \quad \times \sum_{\mathbf{q}}' (-|g_{\text{eff}}|^2) \left( c_{\mathbf{k}' + \mathbf{q}, \uparrow}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf{k}' - \mathbf{q}, \downarrow}^\dagger - c_{-\mathbf{k}' + \mathbf{q}, \uparrow}^\dagger c_{\mathbf{k}' - \mathbf{q}, \downarrow}^\dagger \right) |0\rangle.
    \end{aligned}
\]
To obtain a non-zero vacuum overlap in the interaction bracket, the momenta of the fermions created by the ket must precisely match those destroyed by the bra. This conservation requirement establishes strict algebraic constraints on the allowed momentum transfer vectors \( \mathbf{q} \) (for instance, matching the indices in the cross-terms imposes simultaneous relations such as \( \mathbf{q} = -\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{k}' \) and \( \mathbf{q} = +\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{k}' \), or simply forces a direct transition from pair state \( (\mathbf{k}', -\mathbf{k}') \) to pair state \( (\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k}) \) via the momentum shift \( \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{k} - \mathbf{k}' \)). Consequently, the sum over \( \mathbf{q} \) collapses, leaving only the single scattering channel that connects \( \mathbf{k}' \) to \( \mathbf{k} \). Evaluating the remaining fermionic contractions reduces the total matrix element to a remarkably simple form:
\[
    \langle \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} | H | \Psi \rangle = 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} - |g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \sum_{\mathbf{k}'}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}'}.
\]

\subsubsection{The Consistency Condition and the Cooper Bound State}

Equating the left and right sides of our projected Schrödinger equation, we obtain the explicit eigenvalue equation for the Fourier coefficients \( \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} \):
\[
    E \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} = 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}} \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} - |g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \sum_{\mathbf{k}'}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}'}.
\]
Rearranging the terms to isolate \( \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} \) yields:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cooper_eigenvalue_coeff}
    (E - 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}) \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \sum_{\mathbf{k}'}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}'}.
\end{equation}
Notice that the summation over \( \mathbf{k}' \) on the right-hand side is completely independent of the free index \( \mathbf{k} \); it is simply a constant scalar, which we define as \( C \):
\[
    C = \sum_{\mathbf{k}'}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}'}.
\]
Substituting \( C \) back into the rearranged eigenvalue equation \eqref{eq:cooper_eigenvalue_coeff}, we can explicitly solve for the functional form of the Fourier components \( \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} \):
\[
    \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} = \frac{-|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 C}{E - 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}.
\]
We now insert this expression for \( \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} \) back into the definition of \( C \) by summing both sides over the allowed states \( \mathbf{k} \) to obtain a closed self-consistency equation:
\[
    C = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \varphi_{\mathbf{k}} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \left( \frac{-|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 C}{E - 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}} \right) = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 C \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \frac{1}{E - 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}.
\]
Assuming \( C \neq 0 \) (which physically corresponds to finding a non-trivial bound state solution), we divide both sides by \( C \) to arrive at the fundamental Cooper consistency condition:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cooper_discrete_sum}
    1 = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \frac{1}{E - 2\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}.
\end{equation}

To evaluate this self-consistency sum analytically, we transition to the continuum limit, replacing the discrete sum over momenta \( \mathbf{k} \) with an integral over the single-particle energy \( \epsilon \) weighted by the density of states \( g(\epsilon) \):
\[
    \sum_{\mathbf{k}}' \cdots \xrightarrow{\text{cont. lim.}} \int_0^{\hbar\omega_D} d\epsilon \, g(\epsilon) \cdots
\]
Here, \( g(\epsilon) \) represents the number of available electronic states per unit energy window \( [\epsilon, \epsilon + d\epsilon] \). We Measure the single-particle energies relative to the Fermi level (setting \( \epsilon_F = 0 \)) and integrate strictly from \( 0 \) (the Fermi surface, since states below it are Pauli-blocked by the inert Fermi sea) up to the Debye cutoff energy \( \hbar\omega_D \). This upper limit is enforced because, beyond the Debye frequency, there are no phonon modes available in the crystal lattice to mediate the attractive scattering. Applying the continuum limit to Eq. \eqref{eq:cooper_discrete_sum} gives:
\[
    1 = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 \int_0^{\hbar\omega_D} d\epsilon \, \frac{g(\epsilon)}{E - 2\epsilon}.
\]
Because the relevant phonon integration energy scale (\( \hbar\omega_D \)) is typically orders of magnitude smaller than the electronic Fermi energy (\( E_F \)), the density of states varies negligibly over this narrow shell. We can therefore safely approximate \( g(\epsilon) \) as a constant evaluated precisely at the Fermi surface, \( g(\epsilon) \simeq g(E_F) \):
\[
    1 \simeq -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F) \int_0^{\hbar\omega_D} d\epsilon \, \frac{1}{E - 2\epsilon}.
\]
Evaluating this elementary logarithmic integral yields our implicit algebraic equation for the two-body bound state energy \( E \)\footnote{Note on the integral prefactor: Mathematically, evaluating \( \int_0^{\hbar\omega_D} \frac{d\epsilon}{E - 2\epsilon} \) strictly yields a factor of \( -\frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{E - 2\hbar\omega_D}{E}\right) \), whereas lecture board notes occasionally reflect a coefficient of \( -2 \) (as in \( 1 \simeq -2|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F) \ln(\dots) \)). During the lecture, this discrepancy was addressed verbally as an arbitrary algebraic scaling factor (such as spin-degeneracy factors or density-of-states conventions) that is ultimately absorbed directly into the definition of the dimensionless interaction parameter \( \lambda \). Below, we show the exact \( 1/2 \) integration alongside the lecture's \( \lambda \) parameterization.}:
\[
    1 = -|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F) \left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) \ln\left( \frac{E - 2\hbar\omega_D}{E} \right).
\]

Following the standard parameterization, we define the dimensionless electron-phonon coupling constant \( \lambda \) (absorbing the numerical prefactors into its definition, e.g., \( \frac{1}{\lambda} = -\frac{1}{2|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F)} \) or \( \lambda \equiv |g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F) \) depending on the chosen convention):
\[
    -\frac{1}{\lambda} \simeq \ln\left( \frac{E - 2\hbar\omega_D}{E} \right) \implies e^{-\frac{1}{\lambda}} = 1 - \frac{2\hbar\omega_D}{E},
\]
(or equivalently, keeping the negative sign explicit in the exponent definition as written in the notes, \( 1 - e^{\frac{1}{\lambda}} = \frac{2\hbar\omega_D}{E} \)). Solving explicitly for the energy \( E \), we obtain:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cooper_exact_energy}
    E = \frac{2\hbar\omega_D}{1 - e^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}} = 2\hbar\omega_D \underbrace{\left( \frac{1}{1 - e^{\frac{1}{|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F)}}} \right)}_{< 0} < 0.
\end{equation}
(Note: In the lecture notes, the algebraic rearrangement is also briefly sketched in the simplified product form \( E = 2\hbar\omega_D (1 - e^{1/\lambda}) < 0 \) to immediately highlight the negative sign of the quantity in parentheses).

\subsubsection{Physical Analysis: Weak-Coupling Limit and Temperature Scales}

Let us carefully analyze the physical consequences of Eq. \eqref{eq:cooper_exact_energy}. The dimensionless parameter \( \lambda = |g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F) \) acts as a strictly positive constant measuring the strength of the effective attraction. Since \( \lambda > 0 \), the exponential term \( e^{1/\lambda} \) is strictly greater than \( 1 \), which guarantees that the denominator \( (1 - e^{1/\lambda}) \) is definitively negative. Because the Debye energy \( \hbar\omega_D \) is positive, this mathematically guarantees that \textbf{\( E < 0 \)} (relative to the Fermi surface \( 2\epsilon_F = 0 \)). This is a profound theoretical result: we have rigorously proven that the two-fermion system lowers its energy below the Fermi sea to form a stable \textbf{bound state}, the \textbf{Cooper pair}.

In the physically realistic weak-coupling limit, where the coupling \( \lambda \ll 1 \) and the Debye energy is much larger than the resulting binding energy (\( \hbar\omega_D \gg |E| \)), we can expand the logarithmic relation for \( |E| = -E \):
\[
    \frac{1}{\lambda} \simeq \ln\left( 1 - \frac{2\hbar\omega_D}{E} \right) = \ln\left( 1 + \frac{2\hbar\omega_D}{|E|} \right) \simeq \ln\left( \frac{2\hbar\omega_D}{|E|} \right).
\]
Inverting this asymptotic relation yields the celebrated Cooper formula for the absolute magnitude of the pair binding energy:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cooper_binding_energy}
    |E| = \frac{2\hbar\omega_D}{e^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}} = 2\hbar\omega_D e^{-\frac{1}{\lambda}} = 2\hbar\omega_D \exp\left( -\frac{1}{|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F)} \right).
\end{equation}
(Depending on whether the factor of \( 1/2 \) from the integral is kept explicit rather than absorbed into \( \lambda \), this is equivalently written as \( E = -2\hbar\omega_D e^{-2/\lambda} \)).

This final analytical expression carries two critical physical messages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cooper Instability} \( E < 0 \): We have successfully formed a stable two-electron bound pair. Crucially, unlike in three-dimensional vacuum where an attractive potential must exceed a finite threshold depth to support a bound state, the presence of the Pauli-blocking Fermi sea reduces the effective dimensionality of the density of states to become constant at the Fermi surface. Consequently, \textit{even an arbitrarily weak attractive interaction} (\( \lambda \to 0^+ \)) \textit{produces a bound state}!
    \item \textbf{Non-Perturbative Singularity} \( E \propto e^{-\frac{1}{|g_{\text{eff}}|^2 g(E_F)}} \): The binding energy depends exponentially on the \textit{inverse} of the coupling strength. Mathematically, the function \( e^{-1/\lambda} \) possesses an essential singularity at \( \lambda = 0 \); all of its Taylor series derivatives vanish at zero coupling. From a theoretical standpoint, one might initially be unhappy with this feature because it proves that \uline{the superconducting bound state cannot be reached at any finite order in standard perturbation theory} starting from the normal metal. However, it also naturally explains why the superconducting energy scale is so vastly smaller than the bare electronic scales.
\end{enumerate}

Let us conclude by reasoning about the characteristic temperature and energy scales implied by this hierarchy. The exponential dependence implies that the energy gap created by these bound states is extremely small, meaning the pairs are fragile and can only resist thermal destruction up to very low temperatures. We can roughly estimate the typical order-of-magnitude scales for a conventional metal as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fermi Temperature (\( T_F \sim 10^4\text{ K} \)):} This sets the bare kinetic energy scale of the rapid electron motion in the metal.
    \item \textbf{Effective Phonon Scale (\( g_{\text{eff}} \sim \frac{10^4}{10^2} \sim 10^2\text{ K} \)):} Because the effective attraction arises at second order in electron-phonon perturbation theory (and is bounded by the Debye temperature \( T_D = \hbar\omega_D / k_B \)), the characteristic interaction energy scale drops by roughly two orders of magnitude compared to \( T_F \), landing around \( \sim 100\text{ K} \).
    \item \textbf{Critical Temperature (\( T_c \sim E / k_B \sim \frac{10^2}{10^2} \sim 1\text{ K} \)):} Finally, the non-perturbative exponential suppression \( e^{-1/\lambda} \) reduces the energy scale by another one to two orders of magnitude.
\end{itemize}
Thus, this simple two-body Cooper model provides a completely natural microscopic explanation for why the critical temperatures \( T_c \) observed in conventional metallic superconductors are on the order of \( \sim 1\text{--}10\text{ Kelvin} \), four orders of magnitude smaller than the Fermi temperature. (Note that while there may in principle exist other exotic bound states or excitations at higher energies, our low-energy effective perturbative expansion around the Fermi surface does not allow us to access them).
