\section[Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking and Goldstone Theorem]{Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking and\\Goldstone Theorem}

It is straightforward to verify that the original unperturbed Hamiltonian commutes with the total number operator \( \hat{N} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} a_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger a_{\mathbf{k}} \). This commutation property is intimately connected with the fundamental invariance of the Hamiltonian under global \( \text{U}(1) \) phase transformations:
\[
    a_{\mathbf{k}} \to e^{i\phi} a_{\mathbf{k}},
\]
\[
    a_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger \to e^{-i\phi} a_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger.
\]
These phase rotations are formally realized by the application of the unitary continuous symmetry operator \( U(\phi) \):
\[
    U(\phi) = e^{i\phi\hat{N}}.
\]
Indeed, it is easy to explicitly check the generator action on the annihilation operator:
\[
    U^\dagger(\phi) a_{\mathbf{k}} U(\phi) = e^{i\phi} a_{\mathbf{k}}.
\]
Because the Hamiltonian is invariant under this specific transformation—meaning \( U^\dagger(\phi) H U(\phi) = H \)—Noether's theorem guarantees there should be a conserved macroscopic charge. This conserved charge is nothing else than the mathematical generator of the transformation itself, namely, the total number operator \( \hat{N} \).

Crucially, even if the Hamiltonian governing the system is perfectly invariant under the above \( \text{U}(1) \) transformation, the interacting ground state \( |\psi_0\rangle \) is not. This profound physical phenomenon is known as spontaneous symmetry breaking. There are multiple analytical ways to see this symmetry breaking. Consider the macroscopic expectation value of the zero-momentum annihilation operator \( a_0 \). We previously established that over the coherent ground state, \( \langle \psi_0 | a_0 | \psi_0 \rangle \neq 0 \). Applying the unitary transformation, we have:
\[
    \langle \psi_0 | (U^\dagger(\phi) a_0 U(\phi)) | \psi_0 \rangle = \langle \psi_0 | (e^{i\phi} a_0) | \psi_0 \rangle.
\]
However, if the ground state \( |\psi_0\rangle \) were truly invariant under the symmetry group, then by definition \( U(\phi) |\psi_0\rangle = |\psi_0\rangle \). This invariant assumption would require that the equation evaluates to:
\[
    \langle \psi_0 | a_0 | \psi_0 \rangle = e^{i\phi} \langle \psi_0 | a_0 | \psi_0 \rangle,
\]
which can logically be satisfied for an arbitrary phase \( \phi \) if and only if \( \langle \psi_0 | a_0 | \psi_0 \rangle = 0 \). This immediately leads to a direct contradiction with our foundational result that the condensate is macroscopically occupied. The exact same mathematical proof can be repeated for the pair correlation expectation value at finite momenta \( \mathbf{k} \neq 0 \). In that case, we notice that
\[
    \langle \psi_0 | a_{\mathbf{k}} a_{-\mathbf{k}} | \psi_0 \rangle = -u_k v_k \neq 0,
\]
and applying the operator \( U(\phi) \) to both bare operators and falsely imposing the invariance of the ground state \( |\psi_0\rangle \) once again yields a fatal contradiction.

Once we have rigorously verified that the chosen ground state explicitly breaks the continuous \( \text{U}(1) \) symmetry, we know by Goldstone's theorem that there must emerge massless Goldstone bosons. These excitations must exhibit an energy dispersion that vanishes continuously (specifically, linearly) with the momentum \( p \), yielding \( E \sim cp \) for \( p \to 0 \). Recall that after executing the Bogoliubov transformation, the interacting Hamiltonian becomes diagonalized, featuring the quasiparticle energy spectrum given by \( E_k \). The asymptotic limit \( k \to 0 \) for \( E_k \) has been already evaluated, successfully reproducing exactly the linear phononic dispersion that Goldstone's theorem demands: \( E_k \sim c\hbar k \) (as visualized in Figure \ref{fig:dispersion_asymptotic}), with the characteristic sound velocity \( c \) scaling with the interaction strength.

The derived energy values \( E_k \) structurally describe the dispersion of these elementary excitations, which are collective modes that allow us to describe effectively low-energy many-body states as a simple ideal gas of quasi-particles (see Figure \ref{fig:elementary_excitations}). This quasiparticle framework is particularly relevant at very low temperatures \( T \), when only these low-energy excited states are thermally activated, since random thermal fluctuations are small and strictly of order \( k_B T \).

Consequently, complex interacting states at low energy can be cleanly described as a dilute gas of elementary excitations. It is critical to recognize that these independent excitations are not the original bare bosons created by the standard operators \( a_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger \), but rather new hybridized bosonic modes created by the Bogoliubov operators \( \alpha_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger \) for small \( k \). These elementary excitations are governed by an effective interacting dispersion \( E_k \) that is structurally distinct from the purely quadratic free-particle dispersion \( \epsilon_k \) of the original bare bosons.

Remarkably, even for a strongly interacting bare Bose gas, provided the temperature remains sufficiently low, very few of these hybridized modes are activated. Because the thermal density of quasi-particles is low, these elementary excitations are highly dilute and can thus be treated to an excellent approximation as a non-interacting ideal gas. Lastly, we note a fundamental statistical constraint: for a system comprised exclusively of underlying bosons, like in the present model, only bosonic elementary excitations are physically possible. In contrast, for a many-body system of underlying fermions, the collective excitations can manifest as either fermionic (quasiparticles) or bosonic (e.g., Cooper pairs, plasmons) elementary excitations depending on the specific pairing mechanics.

\section{Specific Heat and Condensate Fraction}

Let us proceed with the calculation of the specific heat of a dilute Bose gas. From the diagonalized Hamiltonian, we established that at absolute zero temperature, \( T = 0 \), the system resides in the quasiparticle vacuum with no elementary excitations. At a finite temperature \( T > 0 \), the thermal mean number of elementary excitations, \( \langle \alpha_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger \alpha_{\mathbf{k}} \rangle \), is governed by the standard Bose-Einstein distribution—since these Bogoliubov quasiparticles are fundamentally bosons:
\[
    \langle \alpha_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger \alpha_{\mathbf{k}} \rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta E_k} - 1},
\]
where \( \beta = (k_B T)^{-1} \). In this expression, the chemical potential \( \mu \) associated with the elementary excitations is strictly zero. This physically arises because the total number of quasiparticle excitations is not a conserved quantity; it is determined dynamically by the thermal equilibrium at temperature \( T \) (entirely analogous to phonons in a crystalline solid or photons in black-body radiation). Consequently, the total internal energy \( E(T) \) of the gas at temperature \( T \) is given by the sum of the zero-point ground state energy and the thermal excitation energy:
\[
    E(T) = \mathcal{E}_0 + \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \frac{E_k}{e^{\beta E_k} - 1}.
\]
Taking the thermodynamic limit, the discrete summation over wave vectors transitions into a continuous integral, yielding the total energy density:
\[
    \frac{E(T)}{V} = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{V} + \int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{E_k}{e^{\beta E_k} - 1}.
\]
For high-energy modes where \( E_k \gtrsim k_B T \), the integrand is exponentially suppressed by the Bose distribution. Therefore, the dominant contribution to the integral comes strictly from the low-energy domain where \( E_k \lesssim k_B T \). At very low temperatures, this restricts our focus to the extreme long-wavelength limit of the dispersion relation.

In this low-momentum regime, we recall that the Bogoliubov dispersion is strictly linear, \( E_k \approx c\hbar k \). Thus, in the asymptotic limit \( T \to 0 \), we can safely replace the full dispersion relation with its phononic approximation inside the integral:
\[
    \frac{E(T)}{V} = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{V} + \int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{c\hbar k}{e^{\beta c\hbar k} - 1}.
\]
Evaluating this integral by switching to spherical coordinates and introducing the dimensionless integration variable \( x = \beta c\hbar k \), we obtain:
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{E(T)}{V} & = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{V} + \left(\frac{k_B T}{c\hbar}\right)^3 k_B T \frac{4\pi}{(2\pi)^3} \int_0^\infty \frac{x^3}{e^x - 1} dx \\
                       & = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{V} + \frac{\pi^2}{30} \frac{(k_B T)^4}{(c \hbar)^3}.
    \end{aligned}
\]

From this energy density, we directly compute the isochoric heat capacity, \( C_V \):
\[
    C_V = \left( \frac{\partial E(T)}{\partial T} \right)_V = \frac{2\pi^2}{15} V k_B \left( \frac{k_B T}{c\hbar} \right)^3.
\]

This final expression reveals that the low-temperature specific heat of the weakly interacting Bose gas scales as \( T^3 \). This characteristic cubic temperature dependence mirrors the behavior of acoustic phonons in a Debye solid or photons in a cavity, fundamentally stemming from the linear phononic dispersion relation. We can starkly contrast this interacting result with the ideal, non-interacting Bose gas, which exhibits a \( T^{3/2} \) dependence due to its purely quadratic free-particle dispersion. In the interacting system, the linear dispersion creates a lower density of states at low energies compared to the non-interacting case, making the elementary excitations significantly harder to thermally excite at temperatures near absolute zero.

\section{Landau Criterion for Superfluidity}

In a normal fluid flowing through a pipe, friction between the fluid and the pipe boundaries leads to a continuous dissipation of energy and a corresponding macroscopic pressure drop along the direction of flow. Conversely, superfluidity (a remarkable quantum phenomenon famously discovered in liquid helium by Pyotr Kapitza) is characterized by the ability of certain fluids to flow without any friction. In a perfectly superfluid state, even at a finite macroscopic flow velocity \( \mathbf{v} > 0 \), the pressure remains exactly constant along the fluid, such that \( \nabla p = 0 \).

Our primary goal is to leverage the spectral properties of the many-body Hamiltonian and the associated elementary excitation dispersion relation to rigorously derive a fundamental condition known as the Landau criterion for superfluidity.

To understand the microscopic origin of friction, consider a classical many-body fluid, such as water, flowing through a pipe. If we assume the system is initially prepared in a state devoid of internal excitations, physical friction with the pipe boundaries will eventually induce their formation. Consequently, the total internal energy of the macroscopic system increases by the cumulative energy of these newly generated thermal excitations. For instance, if the initial state follows a classical Maxwell-Boltzmann momentum distribution, the dissipation of macroscopic forward momentum into random thermal excitations will eventually cause the distribution to narrow and shift toward lower net momenta. This physical picture implies that by examining the energy spectrum and the quasiparticle dispersion relation, we can determine precisely when adding energy to the system through such thermal or frictional processes is kinematically allowed.

Lev Landau proposed an elegant kinematic criterion to establish whether a zero-temperature (\( T = 0 \)) system behaves as a superfluid, specifically allowing one to predict the critical velocity at which the frictionless superfluid flow breaks down. Landau's derivation relies on comparing the energetics of the system in two distinct inertial reference frames: the laboratory frame \( K \), where the fluid moves with a macroscopic velocity \( \mathbf{v} \) and the pipe is perfectly at rest, and the co-moving frame \( K' \), where the fluid is strictly at rest and the pipe moves backwards with a velocity \( -\mathbf{v} \). The frame \( K' \) thus translates with a velocity \( \mathbf{v} \) with respect to the laboratory frame \( K \).

In the co-moving frame \( K' \), the fluid is statically at rest. If the system experiences absolutely no friction with the pipe walls, it remains in its pristine many-body ground state, characterized by the complete absence of thermal excitations (\( \langle \alpha_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger \alpha_{\mathbf{k}} \rangle = 0 \) for all \( \mathbf{k} \)). In this unperturbed scenario, the total energy of the fluid is simply its zero-point ground state energy, \( E'_{(0)} = \mathcal{E}_0 \).

If, however, friction were to occur, the interaction with the moving pipe walls would perturb the fluid, doing work to create elementary excitations. The system's internal energy would consequently increase by \( \sum_{\mathbf{k}} E_k \langle \alpha_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger \alpha_{\mathbf{k}} \rangle \), where the expectation values \( \langle \alpha_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger \alpha_{\mathbf{k}} \rangle \) are positive integers (or strictly non-zero for finite-temperature statistical ensembles). To analyze the threshold of this dissipative process, let us consider the creation of a single elementary excitation with momentum \( \mathbf{p} \). The total energy of the perturbed fluid in frame \( K' \) then becomes \( E'_{(\text{exc})} = \mathcal{E}_0 + E_p \).

We now transform this energy back to the laboratory frame \( K \) using the standard \textbf{Galilean transformation} for the total energy of a many-particle system:
\[
    E = E' + \frac{MV^2}{2} + \mathbf{P}' \cdot \mathbf{V},
\]
where \( E \) is the energy in frame \( K \), \( E' \) and \( \mathbf{P}' \) are the total internal energy and total internal momentum in frame \( K' \), \( M \) is the total macroscopic mass of the fluid, and \( \mathbf{V} \) is the relative velocity of \( K' \) with respect to \( K \). In our setup, this relative frame velocity is exactly \( \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{v} \).

\begin{remark}
    When performing this Galilean boost, one must be exceedingly careful regarding the definition of momentum if the system is coupled to an external gauge field (such as an electromagnetic field for charged superfluids like superconductors). In such cases, the canonical momentum is shifted by the vector potential and does not simply correspond to the inertial mass times the velocity. For our neutral bosonic superfluid, however, this complication does not arise.
\end{remark}

For the fluid hosting a single elementary excitation, its internal energy in \( K' \) is \( E' = E'_{(\text{exc})} = \mathcal{E}_0 + E_p \). Because the macroscopic fluid is at rest in \( K' \), the only contribution to the total internal momentum comes from the newly created excitation itself, so \( \mathbf{P}' = \mathbf{p} \). Substituting these into the transformation yields the total energy of the excited fluid in the laboratory frame \( K \):
\[
    E_{(\text{exc})} = \mathcal{E}_0 + E_p + \frac{Mv^2}{2} + \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{v}.
\]

Conversely, if the fluid remains in the unperturbed ground state, its internal energy in \( K' \) is merely \( E' = E'_{(0)} = \mathcal{E}_0 \), and its total internal momentum is identically zero (\( \mathbf{P}' = 0 \)). Substituting these into the Galilean transformation, the energy of the frictionless flowing fluid in frame \( K \) is simply:
\[
    E_{(0)} = \mathcal{E}_0 + \frac{Mv^2}{2}.
\]

Combining the last two expressions, we determine the energy cost to create the elementary excitation in the laboratory frame:
\[
    E_{(\text{exc})} - E_{(0)} = E_p + \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{v}.
\]

To definitively establish whether the system behaves as a superfluid, we must examine the sign of this energy difference. Friction fundamentally occurs when the creation of an excitation is energetically favorable, which happens when the perturbed state has a lower total energy than the unperturbed flow (\( E_{(\text{exc})} < E_{(0)} \)). In contrast, robust superfluidity is maintained whenever the spontaneous creation of excitations is kinematically forbidden because it costs energy (\( E_{(\text{exc})} > E_{(0)} \)).

The critical velocity \( v_c \) is defined as the minimum macroscopic flow velocity at which the system's total energy can be spontaneously lowered (or kept equal) by the addition of elementary excitations. This threshold dictates exactly when it becomes thermodynamically convenient to dissipate kinetic energy. By imposing the boundary equality \( E_{(\text{exc})} = E_{(0)} \), we will obtain the precise conditions for the critical velocity \( v_c \), which sharply separates the dissipative friction regime from the stable frictionless regime, thereby marking a dynamical phase transition to superfluidity.

In order for the spontaneous creation of an excitation to be kinematically possible in the laboratory reference frame \( K \), the energy difference \( E_{(\text{exc})} - E_{(0)} \) must be negative. This is because friction physically transfers kinetic energy from the moving fluid to the stationary pipe; consequently, the macroscopic fluid must decrease its total energy in this dissipative process. Friction will thus occur only if, for some values of the excitation momentum \( \mathbf{p} \), the following inequality holds:
\[
    E_p + \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{v} < 0.
\]
The above condition is most easily satisfied if the created momentum \( \mathbf{p} \) is aligned exactly anti-parallel (opposite) to the flow velocity \( \mathbf{v} \). In this optimal dissipative configuration, the vector dot product becomes \( -pv \), resulting in the simplified scalar condition:
\[
    \frac{E_p}{p} < v.
\]
So, for a given macroscopic flow velocity \( v \), friction will be physically possible if and only if the above condition is satisfied by some value of the excitation momentum \( p \).

To find the absolute threshold for dissipation, we must consider the global minimum value of the ratio \( E_p/p \) as the momentum \( p \) is varied over all possible accessible states:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:critical_velocity_definition}
    v_c \equiv \min_p \frac{E_p}{p}.
\end{equation}
According to our kinematic inequality, only if the velocity \( v \) of the fluid strictly exceeds this critical threshold \( v_c \) is the creation of excitations (and thus friction) possible. We therefore conclude the fundamental Landau criterion: if \( v_c \) is strictly greater than zero, then the fluid will flow without any friction for all velocities \( v < v_c \), behaving as a true superfluid. Mathematically, at a local minimum of the ratio \( E_p/p \), simple calculus dictates that its derivative must vanish:
\[
    \frac{d}{dp} \left( \frac{E_p}{p} \right) = 0,
\]
yielding the geometric condition:
\[
    \frac{dE_p}{dp} = \frac{E_p}{p}.
\]

Note that this derivative equation has a highly intuitive geometric interpretation: if one plots the excitation energy \( E_p \) as a function of the momentum \( p \), the derivative \( dE_p/dp \) represents the local slope of the tangent to the curve at the point \( (p, E_p) \), while the ratio \( E_p/p \) represents the overall slope of the secant line joining the origin directly to that same point \( (p, E_p) \) (see Figure \ref{fig:landau_geometric}). When these two distinct slopes perfectly coincide, the secant line from the origin becomes tangent to the dispersion curve. It is exactly at this point of tangency that a minimum of \( E_p/p \) occurs, and the numerical value of this tangent slope identically defines the critical velocity \( v_c \).

\begin{example}[No Superfluidity for Ideal BEC]
    Let us apply the Landau criterion to an ideal, non-interacting Bose-Einstein condensate. For a free particle, the energy dispersion is purely kinetic, meaning \( E_p = \frac{p^2}{2m} \sim p^2 \). When evaluating the critical velocity ratio, we find \( \frac{E_p}{p} \propto \frac{p^2}{p} = p \). Taking the limit to find the minimum over all accessible momenta, \( \min_p \left( \frac{E_p}{p} \right) \xrightarrow{p \to 0} 0 \). Because \( v_c = 0 \), any arbitrarily small flow velocity \( v > 0 \) satisfies the condition for dissipation. It is therefore always kinematically convenient to create excitations, meaning an ideal BEC strictly exhibits \uline{no superfluidity}.

For the weakly interacting dilute Bose gas analyzed previously via Bogoliubov theory, recalling the behavior of \( E_p \) for small \( p \), the dispersion is linear: \( E_p \sim cp \). Consequently, the ratio \( E_p/p \) is a constant \( c \) near the origin. The global minimum condition is realized precisely as \( p \to 0 \) (see Figure \ref{fig:interacting_dispersion}):
\[
    v_c = \min_p \frac{E_p}{p} = c.
\]
This profound result means that the weakly interacting Bose gas is a true superfluid for any flow velocity \( v < c \). Only for supersonic flows \( v > c \) are Bogoliubov quasiparticle excitations created, and macroscopic dissipation becomes possible.

Superfluidity is not a universal guarantee; there will always be some critical parameter (such as a threshold velocity or a critical temperature) beyond which the system will spontaneously start to generate excitations and subsequently lose its superfluid properties.

\begin{example}[Helium-4]
    Even in strongly interacting Bose systems that defy simple perturbative treatment, like liquid Helium-4, robust superfluid behavior emerges at sufficiently low temperatures. Finally, let us consider the measured spectrum of elementary excitations for liquid \( ^4\text{He} \) (as determined directly from neutron scattering experiments). We see that in this complex strongly-correlated case, there are actually two distinct geometric solutions (points of tangency) satisfying the Landau condition.

    The tangent line with the absolute lowest slope geometrically corresponds to the true global minimum of \( E_p/p \). In Helium-4, this absolute minimum is reached not at the origin, but at a finite momentum close to the famous "roton minimum" of the dispersion curve \( E_p \). The slope of this tangent gives the corresponding critical velocity for Helium-4: \( v_c \approx 60 \text{ m/s} \). Notice that this critical threshold for creating rotons is significantly lower than the speed of sound determined by the initial linear slope at the origin, which is \( c = 240 \text{ m/s} \).

    The energy gap at this local minimum is called the roton gap, denoted \( \Delta \), and is experimentally measured such that \( \Delta/k_B = 8.7 \text{ K} \). The elementary excitations grouped close to this finite-momentum minimum acquire a distinctly quadratic, particle-like effective form:
    \[
        E_p = \Delta + \frac{(p - p_0)^2}{2m^*}
    \]
    with an effective mass \( m^* = 0.16m \) (where \( m \) is the bare mass of a \( ^4\text{He} \) atom) and a characteristic momentum \( p_0/\hbar = 1.9 \text{ \AA}^{-1} \). These specific high-momentum elementary excitations are called rotons (while the low-momentum excitations corresponding to the initial linear part of the spectrum are standard acoustic phonons). We thus see that when the macroscopic superfluid velocity \( v \) exceeds \( v_c \approx 60 \text{ m/s} \), the spontaneous kinematic excitation of rotons completely destroys the superfluid behavior, even though the flow remains highly subsonic relative to \( c \).
\end{example}

It is crucial to note that the entire Landau derivation presented above assumes absolute zero temperature. If we consider a finite temperature \( T > 0 \), we must take into account the pre-existing gas of thermal excitations that are statistically present in the system. Indeed, even if the kinematic line \( pv \) sits safely below the zero-temperature critical dispersion threshold, we must consider the energy contributions and collisions from these thermal excitations, which can effectively transfer momentum and turn off the macroscopic superfluidity by traversing the dispersion gap. Consequently, the true critical velocity will be strictly lower than at zero temperature, and it will exhibit a strong dependence on the ambient temperature of the system.

Furthermore, at finite temperatures, there will always be a "normal" fraction of the fluid comprised of these thermal excitations that experiences ordinary friction. However, there concurrently exists a remaining fraction of the fluid that remains perfectly superfluid. According to the two-fluid model, the fraction of the fluid that is superfluid scales directly with the total number of particles, ensuring it remains a macroscopically sizable and observable portion of the system up until the critical transition temperature is reached.

\section*{Concluding Remarks: The Physics of Emergence}

Through the mathematical machinery of the Bogoliubov transformation, we systematically mapped the bare bosonic degrees of freedom, \( a_{\mathbf{k}} \), to a new set of operators, \( \alpha_{\mathbf{k}} \). This was not merely a convenient mathematical change of basis, but a profound physical restructuring of the system. The new operators create and destroy collective, hybridized modes that intermix particles and holes. These emergent entities are defined as \textbf{quasiparticles}, and they—rather than the original bare bosons—constitute the true elementary excitations of our interacting many-body description.

This conceptual shift perfectly illustrates the celebrated paradigm articulated by P. W. Anderson: \emph{"More is Different."} Quantum Many-Body Theory (QMBT) demonstrates that macroscopic physics is fundamentally distinct from single-particle quantum mechanics. Merely sticking individual constituent pieces together is entirely insufficient to understand the whole. As the system grows to the thermodynamic limit, non-trivial interactions generate entirely new emergent phenomena that simply do not exist at the level of isolated single particles.

A quintessential example of this macroscopic emergence is \textbf{spontaneous symmetry breaking}, which serves as a foundational pillar of modern QMBT. As we have seen, while the original Hamiltonian inherently respects a continuous global \( \text{U}(1) \) phase symmetry, the macroscopic interacting ground state spontaneously breaks it by developing a definite coherent phase. According to Goldstone's theorem, this broken continuous symmetry mathematically necessitates the emergence of massless Goldstone bosons. In the context of the interacting Bose gas, these Goldstone modes physically manifest as the acoustic phonons characterized by the linear dispersion relation, \( E_k \sim c\hbar k \), at low momenta.

Ultimately, by closely analyzing the experimental observables and the low-energy spectrum of this system, we arrive at a powerful concluding lesson: \textbf{weak interactions can do a lot}. It is tempting to assume that exotic phenomena require strong, non-perturbative couplings. However, we have demonstrated that even an arbitrarily weak interaction is sufficient to completely rewire the vacuum state of the system. A profoundly rich phenomenology—encompassing quantum condensate depletion at absolute zero, the formation of a well-defined sound velocity, spontaneous symmetry breaking, and the onset of macroscopic superfluidity governed by the Landau criterion—emerges not from strong forces, but from the collective many-body nature of weakly interacting quasiparticles.
