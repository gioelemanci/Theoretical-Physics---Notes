\section{Applications of Feynman Diagrams}

Let us now apply the Feynman rules to compute scattering amplitudes for various processes in different quantum field theories. We will start with the simplest case of a scalar field theory and then move on to more complex theories involving fermions and gauge bosons.

We will divide diagrams in two categories:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The tree-level diagrams}: These are the diagrams that do not contain any loops. They represent the leading order contribution to the scattering amplitude and are often the simplest to compute.
          \[
              \feynFourS \qquad \feynFourT \qquad \feynFourU
          \]
    \item \textbf{The loop diagrams}: These are the diagrams that contain one or more loops. They represent higher-order corrections to the scattering amplitude and can be more challenging to compute due to the need for regularization and renormalization techniques.\TODO{improve diagrams.}
          \[
              \feynFourBubble \qquad \feynFourSunset \qquad \feynFourDumbbell
          \]
\end{enumerate}
Thus in the loop diagrams, one or more internal lines form a closed loop, which can lead to unresolved integrals like \(\int \frac{d^4 k}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{1}{k^2 - m^2}\), which is divergent and requires regularization and renormalization to obtain a finite result.

Usually the first type of diagrams (tree-level) gives the dominant contribution to the scattering amplitude, while the second type (loop diagrams) provides corrections that can be important for precision calculations. For the first ones, usually some algebra is enough to compute the amplitude, since we have no integrations left, while for the second ones, one needs to use more advanced techniques to handle the divergences and obtain meaningful results. Loop diagrams can also give rise to interesting phenomena such as virtual particles and vacuum polarization, which can have important implications for the behavior of quantum fields and the structure of spacetime, but they are in general more difficult to solve.

\begin{remark}
    We said that usually at LO only the tree diagrams appears, but this is nothing general: there exist cases in which we have loops appearing alredy at the leading order, which are more complicated.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[\(2 \to  2\) scattering in \(\phi^3\) theory]
    The idea is to compute the \textbf{differential cross section} \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d\sigma}}{\mathrm{d} \Omega}\) for the \(2 \to 2\) scattering process of identical particles in \(\phi^3\) theory at tree level, in the center of mass frame, as a function of the total center of mass energy \(E\) and the scattering angle \(\theta\).

    At the leading order, we have already computed the matrix element for the \(2 \to 2\) scattering process in \(\phi^3\) theory: the Feynman rules told us that the relevant diagrams at tree level include the s-channel, t-channel, and u-channel diagrams:
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
            i \mathcal{M} & = \feynFourS + \feynFourT + \feynFourU + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^4)                                                                               \\
                          & = (-i \lambda)^2 \left[ \frac{1}{(p_1 + p_2)^2 - m^2} + \frac{1}{(p_1 - p_3)^2 - m^2} + \frac{1}{(p_1 - p_4)^2 - m^2} \right] + O(\lambda^4).
        \end{aligned}
    \]

    We can define the \textbf{scattering angle} \(\theta\) as the angle between the incoming particle with momentum \(p_1\) and the outgoing particle with momentum \(p_3\):
    \[
        \cos \theta = \frac{\mathbf{p}_1 \cdot \mathbf{p}_3}{|\mathbf{p}_1||\mathbf{p}_3|},
    \]
    and this will allow us to express the scattering amplitude in terms of the Mandelstam variables, which are functions of \(E\) and \(\theta\). Before proceeding with the calculation, we have to study in more detail the kinematics of the process, which is the subject of the next section.
\end{example}

\subsection{4-Point Kinematics}

Consider a more general \(2 \to 2\) scattering process, involving two incoming particles with momenta \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), and two outgoing particles with momenta \(p_3\) and \(p_4\). We are not interested in the specific theory describing the interaction or the type of particles involved, but we are requesting that all particles are on-shell, so that
\[
    p_i^2 = m^2, \quad i = 1, 2, 3, 4.
\]
The scattering amplitude is lorentz invariant, so that it is useful to express it in terms of the \textbf{Mandelstam variables} \(s\), \(t\), and \(u\).

\begin{definition}[Mandelstam variables]
    The \textbf{Mandelstam variables} \(s\), \(t\), and \(u\), are defined as follows:
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
            s & = (p_1 + p_2)^2 = (p_3 + p_4)^2, \\
            t & = (p_1 - p_3)^2 = (p_4 - p_2)^2, \\
            u & = (p_1 - p_4)^2 = (p_2 - p_3)^2.
        \end{aligned}
    \]
    These variables are not independent, since they satisfy the relation
    \[
        s + t + u = \sum_{j} m_j^2,
    \]
    as we will later prove. These variables are defined in such a way that they are invariant under Lorentz transformations, and provide a convenient way to express the scattering amplitude in terms of the kinematic variables of the process.
\end{definition}

Now the idea is to express the scattering amplitude in terms of the Mandelstam variables, which will allow us to compute the differential cross section as a function of the scattering angle \(\theta\) and the total center of mass energy \(E\).

Starting from their definitions, we can express
\[
    s = p_1^2 + p_2^2 + 2 p_1 \cdot p_2 = m_1^2 + m_2^2 + 2 p_1 \cdot p_2,
\]
so that it becomes clear that the Mandelstam variables are related to the Lorentz invariants \(p_i \cdot p_j\). In particular, we have
\[
    \begin{aligned}
         & \begin{dcases}
               p_1 \cdot p_2 = \frac{1}{2}\left( s - m_1^2 - m_2^2 \right), \\
               p_1 \cdot p_3 = \frac{1}{2}\left( t - m_1^2 - m_3^2 \right), \\
               p_2 \cdot p_3 = \frac{1}{2}\left( u - m_2^2 - m_3^2 \right), \\
               p_4 = p_1 + p_2 - p_3,
           \end{dcases}
    \end{aligned}
\]
so that every Lorentz invariant \(p_i \cdot p_j\) can be expressed in terms of the Mandelstam variables, since \(p_4\) can be always expressed in terms of the other three momenta using momentum conservation. Thus we are going to square the last relation, and we will obtain
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        m_4^2 = p_4^2 & = (p_1 + p_2 - p_3)^2 = m_1^2 + m_2^2 + m_3^2 + 2 p_1 \cdot p_2 - 2 p_1 \cdot p_3 - 2 p_2 \cdot p_3                            \\
                      & = m_1^2 + m_2^2 + m_3^2 + 2 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\left( s - m_1^2 - m_2^2 \right)                                          \\
                      & - 2 \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\left( t - m_1^2 - m_3^2 \right) - 2 \left(- \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\left( u - m_2^2 - m_3^2 \right) \\
                      & = s + t + u - m_1^2 - m_2^2 - m_3^2.
    \end{aligned}
\]
Thus we have
\[
    s + t + u = \sum_{j} m_j^2,
\]
which is the relation we wanted to prove. For an \(N\)-particle scattering process, we can define the Mandelstam variables as (\(N>4\)):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \(4N\) momentum components \(p_i^\mu\) for \(i = 1, \dots, N\).
    \item \(N\) on-shell conditions \(p_i^2 = m_i^2\).
    \item \(4\) momentum conservation conditions \(\sum_{i=1}^N p_i^\mu = 0\) (from translational invariance).
    \item 6 independent Lorentz invariants \(p_i \cdot p_j\) for \(i < j\), i.e. 6 lorentz transformations leaving the amplitude invariant.
\end{enumerate}
Thus in the end we have
\[
    4N - N - 4 - 6 = 3N - 10 \textbf{ independent invariants},
\]
which can be chosen as
\[
    s_{ij} = (p_i + p_j)^2, \quad i < j,
\]
these represent the generalization of the Mandelstam variables for \(N\)-particle scattering processes.

\subsubsection{Applications}

Now for a \(2 \to  2\) general process, in the center of mass frame, we have two incoming particles parallel along the \(z\)-axis, and two outgoing particles with momenta \(\mathbf{p}_3\) and \(\mathbf{p}_4\) forming an angle \(\theta\) with the \(z\)-axis
\[
    p_1 = \begin{pmatrix}
        E_1 \\
        0   \\
        0   \\
        p
    \end{pmatrix}, \quad p_2 = \begin{pmatrix}
        E_2 \\
        0   \\
        0   \\
        -p
    \end{pmatrix},
\]
where \(p = \vert \mathbf{p}_1 \vert = \vert \mathbf{p}_2 \vert \) since \(\mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2 = 0\) in the center of mass frame. Now considering the outgoing particles, we can rotate the coordinate system so that they lie in the \(z\)-\(y\)-plane, with momenta
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf{p}_4 = \mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2 - \mathbf{p}_3 = - \mathbf{p}_3, \quad p^{\prime} = \vert \mathbf{p}_3 \vert = \vert \mathbf{p}_4 \vert, \\
        \mathbf{p}_3 = \begin{pmatrix}
                           E_3                    \\
                           0                      \\
                           p^{\prime} \sin \theta \\
                           p^{\prime} \cos \theta
                       \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{p}_4 = \begin{pmatrix}
                                                               E_4                     \\
                                                               0                       \\
                                                               -p^{\prime} \sin \theta \\
                                                               -p^{\prime} \cos \theta
                                                           \end{pmatrix},
    \end{aligned}
\]
since we are still in the center of mass frame; \(\theta\) is the scattering angle defined previously. Note that the scattering amplitude is invariant under rotations, so that we can always choose the coordinate system in such a way that the outgoing particles lie in the \(z\)-\(y\)-plane, and this will not affect the final result for the scattering amplitude.

Now let us compute the center of mass energy \(E\) and the Mandelstam variables in terms of the scattering angle \(\theta\). We have
\[
    E \equiv E_{cm} = E_1 + E_2 = E_3 + E_4,
\]
with the first Mandelstam variable defined as
\[
    s = (p_1 + p_2)^2 = (E_1 + E_2)^2 = E^2,
\]
since the spatial momenta of the incoming particles cancel out. Now
\[
    (p_1 + p_2) \cdot p_1 = E \, E_1,
\]
and we can also express it as
\[
    (p_1 + p_2) \cdot p_1 = m_1^2 + p_2 \cdot p_1 = m_1^2 + \frac{1}{2}(s - m_1^2 - m_2^2) = \frac{1}{2}(E^2 + m_1^2 - m_2^2).
\]
Thus we have
\[
    \begin{dcases}
        E_1 = \frac{E^2 + m_1^2 - m_2^2}{2E}, \\
        E_2 = \frac{E^2 + m_2^2 - m_1^2}{2E}, \\
    \end{dcases}
\]
and now
\[
    m_1^2 = p_1^2 = E_1 - \vert \mathbf{p}_1 \vert^2, \quad p = \frac{\sqrt{\left[E^2 - (m_1 + m_2)^2\right]\left[E^2 - (m_1 - m_2)^2\right]}}{2E},
\]
obtaining the expression for the momentum of the incoming particles in the center of mass frame. The problem is symmetric in the incoming and outgoing particles, so that we can do the same calculation for the outgoing particles, and we will obtain
\[
    \begin{dcases}
        E_3 = \frac{E^2 + m_3^2 - m_4^2}{2E}, \\
        E_4 = \frac{E^2 + m_4^2 - m_3^2}{2E},
    \end{dcases} \quad p^{\prime} = \frac{\sqrt{\left[E^2 - (m_3 + m_4)^2\right]\left[E^2 - (m_3 - m_4)^2\right]}}{2E},
\]
where the same calculation enforces the substitutions \(1 \to 3\), \(2 \to 4\) and \(p \to p^{\prime}\).

For the second Mandelstam variable, we have similarly
\[
    t = (p_1 - p_3)^2 = m_1^2 + m_3^2 - 2 p_1 \cdot p_3 = m_1^2 + m_2^2 - 2(E_1 E_3 - p p^{\prime} \cos \theta) = t(E, \theta),
\]
showing that \(t\) is a function of the center of mass energy \(E\) and the scattering angle \(\theta\). For the third Mandelstam variable, we have
\[
    u = (p_1 - p_4)^2 = m_1^2 + m_4^2 - 2 p_1 \cdot p_4 = m_1^2 + m_4^2 - 2(E_1 E_4 + p p^{\prime} \cos \theta) = u(E, \theta),
\]
which is also a function of the invariants \(E\) and \(\theta\). Thus we have expressed the Mandelstam variables in terms of the center of mass energy \(E\) and the scattering angle \(\theta\), which will allow us to compute the scattering amplitude and the differential cross section as a function of these variables.

\subsubsection{Special cases}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \((m_1 = m_2)\): In this case, we have
          \[
              E_1 = E_2 = \frac{E}{2}, \quad p = \frac{\sqrt{E^2 - 4 m^2}}{2}.
          \]
    \item \((m_3 = m_4)\): In this case, we have
          \[
              E_3 = E_4 = \frac{E}{2}, \quad p^{\prime} = \frac{\sqrt{E^2 - 4 m^2}}{2}.
          \]
    \item \textbf{Elastic scattering}: In this case, we have \(m_1 = m_3\) and \(m_2 = m_4\), so that
          \[
              E_1 = E_3, \quad E_2 = E_4, \quad p = p^{\prime}.
          \]
    \item \((m_1 = m_2 = m_3 = m_4)\): In this case, we have
          \[
              E_i = \frac{E}{2}, \quad p = p^{\prime} = \frac{\sqrt{E^2 - 4 m^2}}{2}.
          \]
          This is the combination of the previous two cases, and it is the most common one in practice, since it describes the scattering of identical particles. The mandelstam variables in this case are given by
          \[
              \begin{dcases}
                  s = E^2,                                         \\
                  t = -\tfrac{1}{2}(E^2 - 4 m^2)(1 - \cos \theta), \\
                  u = -\tfrac{1}{2}(E^2 - 4 m^2)(1 + \cos \theta),
              \end{dcases}
          \]
          with the usual relation \(s + t + u = 4 m^2\).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Back to The Example}

Now we have what we need to compute the scattering amplitude for the \(2 \to 2\) scattering process in \(\phi^3\) theory at tree level, which is given by the sum of the s-channel, t-channel, and u-channel diagrams. Let us come back to this example.

\begin{example}[\(2 \to  2\) scattering in \(\phi^3\) theory]
    Remember that at the leading order, the matrix element for the \(2 \to 2\) scattering process in \(\phi^3\) theory can be computed using the Feynman rules: the relevant Feynman diagrams at tree level include the s-channel, t-channel, and u-channel diagrams, which have the following momentum dependence:
    \[
        i \mathcal{M} = \feynFourS + \feynFourT + \feynFourU + O(\lambda^4),
    \]
    which contribute to the scattering amplitude as follows:
    \[
        i \mathcal{M} = (-i \lambda)^2 \left[ \frac{1}{s - m^2} + \frac{1}{t - m^2} + \frac{1}{u - m^2} \right] + O(\lambda^4),
    \]
    from which it is easy to understand why they are called s-channel, t-channel, and u-channel diagrams; remember that in this case the Mandelstam variables are given by
    \[
        \begin{dcases}
            s = E^2,                                         \\
            t = -\tfrac{1}{2}(E^2 - 4 m^2)(1 - \cos \theta), \\
            u = -\tfrac{1}{2}(E^2 - 4 m^2)(1 + \cos \theta),
        \end{dcases}
    \]
    since we are considering the scattering of identical particles, so that \(m_1 = m_2 = m_3 = m_4 = m\).

    Now we can express the scattering amplitude in terms of the Mandelstam variables (expressing them in terms of \(E\) and \(\theta\) as we found before), which will allow us to compute the differential cross section as a function of the scattering angle \(\theta\) and the total center of mass energy \(E\).

    In particular, we have the differential cross section found at the beginning of the section, which in the center of mass frame can be expressed as
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
            \frac{\mathrm{d\sigma}}{\mathrm{d} \Omega} & = \frac{1}{64 \pi^2 E^2} \frac{p^{\prime}}{p} |\mathcal{M}|^2 = \frac{1}{64 \pi^2 E^2} \frac{\sqrt{E^2 - 4 m^2}}{\sqrt{E^2 - 4 m^2}} |\mathcal{M}|^2             \\
                                                       & = \frac{1}{64 \pi^2 s} |\mathcal{M}|^2 = \frac{\lambda^4}{64 \pi^2 s} \left| \frac{1}{s - m^2} + \frac{1}{t - m^2} + \frac{1}{u - m^2} \right|^2 + O(\lambda^6).
        \end{aligned}
    \]
    This is the final result for the differential cross section for the \(2 \to 2\) scattering process in \(\phi^3\) theory at tree level, as a function of the total center of mass energy \(E\) and the scattering angle \(\theta\).
\end{example}

We can generalize some of the Feynman rules we have found for the real scalar field to other theories, such as for different types of fields (complex scalar fields, Dirac fermion fields, vector boson fields) and for different interactions (Yukawa interactions, gauge interactions).

For example, the generalization to a \(\phi^n\) theory is basically trivial:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_\text{int} = - \frac{\lambda}{n!} \phi^n,
\]
we just have to take into account the fact that each interaction vertex will now involve \(n\) lines instead of 3, and the Feynman rule for the vertex will be given by \(-i \lambda\) times a combinatorial factor that takes into account the number of ways to connect the lines to the vertex.

\begin{remark}
    Remember that the Feynman rules for vertices are rigorously derived from the action integral $\int d^4x \, i\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}(x)$ after performing all possible Wick contractions between the fields in the interaction Lagrangian and the external states (or internal propagators).
\end{remark}

For example, for a \(2 \to 2\) process in a \(\phi^4\) theory, since the vertexes come from the integrals \(\int \mathrm{d}^4 x \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}\) after contracting all the fields, at the tree level we have only one diagram:
\[
    i \mathcal{M} = \feynFourTree + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^2) = -i \lambda + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^2),
\]
which is the one with a single vertex, and the Feynman rule for the vertex will be given by \(-i \lambda\).

\subsubsection{Interactions Involving Derivatives}

A physically richer and mathematically more subtle case arises when the interaction Lagrangian contains derivatives of the fields. To illustrate this, let us consider a toy model with the following interaction term:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} = -\frac{\lambda}{2!} \phi (\partial_\mu \phi)(\partial^\mu \phi)
\]
Note the symmetry factor $\frac{1}{2!}$: permuting the two identical $(\partial_\mu \phi)$ terms leaves the operator unchanged, but the plain field $\phi$ without derivatives plays a distinct mathematical role.

To derive the momentum-space Feynman rule for this vertex, we recall that the spacetime integrals $\int d^4x$ yield the overall momentum conservation delta functions. When we evaluate the Wick contractions, the fields $\phi(x)$ are replaced by their Fourier modes. The spacetime derivative $\partial_\mu$ acting on a plane wave $e^{\pm ip \cdot x}$ pulls down a factor of momentum.

Specifically, the general prescription for translating spacetime derivatives into momentum space is:
\[
    \partial_\mu \to
    \begin{cases}
        +i p_\mu & \text{for OUTGOING momenta} \\
        -i p_\mu & \text{for INCOMING momenta}
    \end{cases}
\]

\begin{remark}
    In this simplified derivation, we are conveniently ignoring subtle issues regarding the strict time-ordering ($T$-product) of derivative operators inside the path integral or Dyson series. While a completely rigorous derivation requires more care (often involving the Hamiltonian formalism or path integral subtleties), the resulting momentum-space Feynman rule obtained via this simple substitution is indeed correct.
\end{remark}

Let us apply this to our toy model to find the vertex rule for a $1 \to 2$ process, with one incoming momentum $p_1$ and two outgoing momenta $p_2, p_3$.
\TODO{add diagram showing a 3-point vertex with one incoming line p1 and two outgoing lines p2, p3}

The fully contracted term in the amplitude involves the integral:
\[
    \approx i \int d^4x \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} = \frac{-i\lambda}{2!} \int d^4x \, e^{-ip_1 \cdot x} \left( \partial_\mu e^{+ip_2 \cdot x} \right) \left( \partial^\mu e^{+ip_3 \cdot x} \right) + \text{permutations}
\]
When the derivatives act on the outgoing plane waves, they pull down factors of $(+ip_2)$ and $(+ip_3)$. We must also sum over all possible ways to contract the external momenta $(p_1, p_2, p_3)$ with the specific fields in the Lagrangian $(\phi, \partial_\mu \phi, \partial^\mu \phi)$.

Evaluating the sum over permutations:
\begin{align*}
     & \frac{-i\lambda}{2!} \Big[
    (ip_{2\mu})(ip_3^\mu) \times 2 \quad (\text{from permuting } p_2 \leftrightarrow p_3 \text{ on the derivative fields})  \\
     & + (-ip_{1\mu})(ip_2^\mu) \times 2 \quad (\text{from contracting } p_1 \text{ and } p_2 \text{ with the derivatives}) \\
     & + (-ip_{1\mu})(ip_3^\mu) \times 2 \quad (\text{from contracting } p_1 \text{ and } p_3 \text{ with the derivatives})
        \Big] \times (2\pi)^4 \delta^{(4)}(p_1 - p_2 - p_3)
\end{align*}
Simplifying the algebra, the explicit Feynman rule for this derivative vertex is:
\[
    \text{Vertex}(p_1, p_2, p_3) = (-i\lambda) \big[ -(p_2 \cdot p_3) + (p_1 \cdot p_2) + (p_1 \cdot p_3) \big]
\]

\subsubsection{Crossing Symmetry}
Notice that in the momentum space rules, swapping an incoming particle with an outgoing particle is mathematically equivalent to flipping the overall sign of its momentum vector ($p \to -p$). This is a specific manifestation of a very deep and important principle in QFT known as \textbf{Crossing Symmetry}:
\begin{quote}
    An incoming particle with momentum $p$ and polarization $s$ is mathematically equivalent to an outgoing anti-particle with momentum $-p$ and polarization $-s$ in the evaluation of scattering matrix elements.
\end{quote}


\section{General Approach for Feynman Rules}

As a more general and robust approach for finding Feynman rules, we should analyze the direct effect of the interaction operator $i \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}$ on the quantum states. This approach is powerful because it applies equally well to virtual particles where $p^2 \neq m^2$.

Let us explicitly recall the operator action for a complex scalar field:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\hat{\phi} = \hat{\phi}(\hat{a}, \hat{b}^\dagger) \implies$ DESTROYS incoming particles OR CREATES outgoing anti-particles.
    \item $\hat{\phi}^\dagger = \hat{\phi}(\hat{a}^\dagger, \hat{b}) \implies$ DESTROYS incoming anti-particles OR CREATES outgoing particles.
\end{itemize}
(For a real scalar field, particles and anti-particles coincide, so $\hat{\phi} = \hat{\phi}^\dagger$).

We can formally define contractions directly between the field operators in the Lagrangian and the physical external states:
\[
    \contraction{}{\hat{\phi}}{}{| p^{\phi}\rangle} \hat{\phi} | p^{\phi} \rangle, \quad \contraction{}{\langle p^{\bar{\phi}} |}{}{\hat{\phi}} \langle p^{\bar{\phi}} | \hat{\phi},
\]
\[
    \contraction{}{\hat{\phi}^\dagger}{}{| p^{\bar{\phi}}\rangle} \hat{\phi}^\dagger | p^{\bar{\phi}} \rangle, \quad \contraction{}{\langle p^{\phi} |}{}{\hat{\phi}^\dagger} \langle p^{\phi} | \hat{\phi}^\dagger,
\]
Here, $|p^{\phi}\rangle$ denotes a state containing a particle, and $|p^{\bar{\phi}}\rangle$ denotes a state containing an anti-particle. Importantly, when applying this technique to derive vertex rules, these states do not strictly need to be on-shell; we are merely extracting the algebraic effect of the operators $\hat{\phi}, \hat{\phi}^\dagger$ inside the Feynman diagrams.

To find the vertex rule, one computes the matrix element of the interaction Hamiltonian density between the desired external states and sums over all possible allowed non-zero Wick contractions.

Returning to our previous derivative toy model $\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} = -\frac{\lambda}{2!} \phi (\partial_\mu \phi)(\partial^\mu \phi)$ (where $\phi$ is real, so $\phi = \phi^\dagger$), we evaluate the matrix element:
\[
    \langle p_2, p_3 | \frac{-i\lambda}{2!} \hat{\phi} (\partial_\mu \hat{\phi})(\partial^\mu \hat{\phi}) | p_1 \rangle.
\]
Applying Wick's theorem, we contract the operators with the states. For example, one specific contraction pairing is:
\[
    \frac{-i\lambda}{2!} \bcontraction[0.5ex]{\langle p_2, p_3 |}{\hat{\phi}}{(\partial_\mu \hat{\phi})(\partial^\mu \hat{\phi}) |}{p_1} \bcontraction[1ex]{\langle}{p_2}{, p_3 | \hat{\phi} (\partial_\mu}{\hat{\phi}} \contraction{\langle p_2,}{p_3}{| \hat{\phi} (\partial_\mu \hat{\phi})(\partial^\mu}{\hat{\phi}} \langle p_2, p_3 | \hat{\phi} (\partial_\mu \hat{\phi})(\partial^\mu \hat{\phi}) | p_1 \rangle.
\]
This specific contraction yields:
\[
    = \left(\frac{-i\lambda}{2!}\right) (i p_{2\mu}) (i p_3^\mu) \times 2!
\]
The $2!$ symmetry factor arises because permuting the two final states $p_2 \leftrightarrow p_3$ into the two indistinguishable derivative operators $(\partial_\mu \phi)(\partial^\mu \phi)$ gives an identical mathematical result. Summing this result with all other valid permutations of the momenta into the different field operators reproduces the exact same vertex rule derived previously.

\subsection{Complex Scalar Field}

For a complex scalar field, the total Lagrangian is given by \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{free}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}\). Unlike the real scalar field, the field operator \(\phi\) and its conjugate \(\phi^*\) (or \(\phi^\dagger\)) are distinct. We recall their effects on quantum states:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\phi = \phi(a, b^\dagger)\) destroys incoming particles or creates outgoing anti-particles.
    \item \(\phi^* = \phi(a^\dagger, b)\) destroys incoming anti-particles or creates outgoing particles.
\end{itemize}

The Feynman propagator is defined as the time-ordered product:
\[
    D_F(x_1, x_2) = \langle 0 | T \{ \phi^*(x_1) \phi(x_2) \} | 0 \rangle
\]
Because \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are not equivalent in this expectation value, we must place an \textbf{arrow} on the propagator line from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\).
\TODO{add diagram: solid line from x1 to x2 with an arrow pointing towards x2}

By writing \(\phi\) and \(\phi^*\) in terms of creation and annihilation operators (\(a, a^\dagger, b, b^\dagger\)), one can physically interpret this propagator as either a particle propagating from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), or an anti-particle propagating backward from \(x_2\) to \(x_1\).

\begin{remark}
    This leads to a fundamental convention in Feynman diagrams (consistently with crossing symmetry):
    An \textbf{INCOMING ARROW} towards a vertex can represent either an \textit{incoming particle} or an \textit{outgoing anti-particle}. Conversely, an \textbf{OUTGOING ARROW} from a vertex represents either an \textit{outgoing particle} or an \textit{incoming anti-particle}.
\end{remark}

Consider the quartic interaction for a complex scalar field:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} = -\frac{\lambda}{4} |\phi|^4 = -\lambda \left( \frac{\phi^2}{2!} \frac{\phi^{*2}}{2!} \right)
\]
Note that there is no \(4!\) factorial in the denominator, but rather a factor of 4 (which can be split into \(2! \times 2!\) to account for the symmetries of \(\phi\) and \(\phi^*\) independently).
The corresponding Feynman rule is simply \(-i\lambda\) for a vertex with exactly 2 incoming arrows and 2 outgoing arrows.

\begin{example}[Two different types of particles in sQED]
    Consider an extension of sQED containing two distinct, minimally coupled complex scalar fields, $\phi_A$ and $\phi_B$, with masses $m_A$ and $m_B$ respectively.
    \[
        \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}^2 + |D_\mu \phi_A|^2 - m_A^2|\phi_A|^2 + |D_\mu \phi_B|^2 - m_B^2|\phi_B|^2
    \]
    Let us compute the tree-level amplitude for the annihilation process $\phi_A(p_1) \bar{\phi}_A(p_2) \to \phi_B(p_3) \bar{\phi}_B(p_4)$.
    Because $\phi_A$ and $\phi_B$ are distinct fields, they only interact through the exchange of a photon. Thus, there is only one $s$-channel diagram contributing at tree-level.
    \TODO{add diagram: s-channel diagram with incoming A scalars, exchanging a photon, and outgoing B scalars}

    We assign Lorentz indices $\mu$ and $\nu$ to the ends of the internal photon propagator. Applying the sQED 3-point vertex rules:
    \[
        i\mathcal{M} = \left[ -ie(p_1^\mu - p_2^\mu) \right] \frac{-i g_{\mu\nu}}{(p_1 + p_2)^2} \left[ -ie(p_3^\nu - p_4^\nu) \right]
    \]
    \begin{remark}
        Notice the crucial \textbf{minus sign} in the momentum factor $(-p_2^\mu)$ for the incoming anti-particle. This is a direct application of the \textbf{Crossing Rule}: the rule for the vertex is $-ie(p_{\text{in}} + p_{\text{out}})$. Since $p_2$ is an incoming anti-particle, we must treat it as an outgoing particle with momentum $-p_2$, hence the minus sign. The same logic applies to the outgoing anti-particle $p_4$, which yields $(-p_4^\nu)$.
    \end{remark}

    Contracting the indices with the metric tensor and using Mandelstam variables ($s = (p_1+p_2)^2$):
    \[
        i\mathcal{M} = \frac{ie^2}{s} (p_1 \cdot p_3 - p_1 \cdot p_4 - p_2 \cdot p_3 + p_2 \cdot p_4)
    \]
    Using kinematic identities (e.g., $p_1 \cdot p_3 = p_2 \cdot p_4 = -\frac{1}{2}(t - m_A^2 - m_B^2)$ and $p_1 \cdot p_4 = p_2 \cdot p_3 = -\frac{1}{2}(u - m_A^2 - m_B^2)$), this beautifully simplifies to:
    \[
        i\mathcal{M} = ie^2 \frac{u - t}{s} \qquad (\text{with } s+t+u = 2m_A^2 + 2m_B^2)
    \]

    \textit{Kinematic Note:} Momentum conservation implies that the center-of-mass energy $\sqrt{s} = E_{\text{c.m.}} = E_3 + E_4 \geq 2m_B$. Therefore, if $m_A < m_B$, this reaction is only physically possible if the initial particles $\phi_A, \bar{\phi}_A$ are accelerated to a sufficiently high center-of-mass energy ($\sqrt{s} \geq 2m_B$) to overcome the mass threshold of the heavier $B$ particles.
\end{example}

\subsection{Observations on Diagrams}

Feynman diagrams are fundamental mathematical tools used to make perturbative predictions in Quantum Field Theory (QFT). Each diagram represents a specific mathematical contribution to a Green function or an element of the scattering matrix ($S$-matrix).

It is crucial to understand that Feynman diagrams are \textbf{not physical objects}. You cannot measure or experimentally observe a single Feynman diagram; only their coherent sum corresponds to a measurable physical probability. Furthermore, a single diagram generally does \textbf{not} respect the fundamental symmetries of the underlying theory (such as gauge invariance or Bose/Fermi symmetry). Only the complete sum of all diagrams at a given perturbative order perfectly restores these physical symmetries.

Despite these strict mathematical realities, it remains extremely common and useful to assign a heuristic physical meaning to the components of these diagrams to guide our physical intuition (provided one keeps the aforementioned caveats in mind). We can classify the components into two distinct categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{External Lines (Real Particles):} These represent the actual external particles associated with the incoming and outgoing asymptotic states.
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Their momenta are strictly \textbf{on-shell}, meaning they satisfy the relativistic energy-momentum relation $p_i^2 = m_i^2$.
              \item They are considered to live for a \textit{long time} (macroscopic distances) compared to the microscopic time scale of the quantum interaction.
          \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Internal Lines (Virtual Particles):} These mathematically correspond to the propagators bridging the interaction vertices.
          \begin{itemize}
              \item They represent intermediate particles that live for a very \textit{short time}.
              \item Their momenta are strictly \textbf{off-shell}, meaning $p_i^2 \neq m_i^2$.
              \item In tree-level diagrams, the 4-momentum of a virtual particle is entirely fixed by momentum conservation at the connected vertices.
              \item In loop diagrams, the internal momenta are not uniquely fixed by conservation laws, meaning we must "sum" (integrate) over all possible momentum states via $\int d^4k$.
              \item Virtual particles, regardless of whether they appear in trees or loops, only "exist" as intermediate transition states and are \textbf{never directly observed}.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

As an illustrative example, consider the annihilation process $\phi_A \bar{\phi}_A \to \phi_B \bar{\phi}_B$ in scalar Quantum Electrodynamics (sQED).

In this specific diagram, the initial $\phi_A$ states are the "prepared incoming states" and the final $\phi_B$ states are the "observed outgoing states". The internal wavy line is the mediating \textbf{virtual photon}. Because it is short-lived and off-shell ($p^2 \neq m_\gamma^2 = 0$), it is inherently unobservable.

\begin{remark}
    A profound consequence of quantum mechanics is that virtual particles can appear in Feynman diagrams—and thus indirectly, but measurably, affect our observations of a process—even if the system does not possess enough center-of-mass energy to produce them as actual, real particles.
\end{remark}

\section{Crossing Symmetry}

We have seen that Feynman rules obey a fundamental property known as \textbf{Crossing Symmetry}. Mathematically, this principle states an equivalence in the evaluation of scattering matrix elements:
\begin{quote}
    INCOMING $\phi_s(p) \iff$ OUTGOING $\bar{\phi}_{-s}(-p)$
\end{quote}
where $\phi_s(p)$ represents a particle with momentum $p$ and polarization state $s$, and $\bar{\phi}$ represents its corresponding anti-particle.

This powerful symmetry allows us to write just \textbf{one} Feynman rule per interaction term, rather than having to define separate rules for every possible combination of incoming and outgoing particles. For example, in scalar QED, the fundamental 3-point vertex rule is $-ie(p_1^\mu + p_2^\mu)$ for two incoming scalars.
By applying crossing symmetry, this single rule automatically implies the rule for a process with one incoming particle ($p_1$) and one outgoing particle ($p_2$), which simply becomes $-ie(p_1^\mu - p_2^\mu)$, and so on. We only need to write one generic combination, and all others are implicitly derived.

\TODO{add diagram: showing the basic sQED 3-point vertex with two incoming scalars p1, p2, and a second diagram showing the crossed version with p1 incoming and p2 outgoing}

Because full scattering amplitudes $\mathcal{M}$ are constructed purely from these symmetric Feynman rules, it logically follows that the scattering amplitudes themselves strictly obey crossing symmetry.

\begin{remark}
    This statement is also a direct corollary of the LSZ reduction formula. When applied to a complete amplitude, crossing symmetry must be understood mathematically as an \textbf{Analytic Continuation}. Physical scattering processes only make sense for external particles with strictly positive energy ($p^0 > 0$). However, because the amplitudes are analytic functions of the external momenta, we can mathematically continue these functions into the unphysical region where $p^0 < 0$. This analytic continuation is precisely what validates the transformation $p \to -p$ used in crossing.
\end{remark}

Thanks to crossing symmetry, once we have computed the analytic expression of an amplitude for a specific process, we can obtain the amplitudes for all related crossed processes essentially "for free".

\begin{example}[Crossing in sQED]
    Suppose we have already computed the amplitude for the annihilation process $\phi_A \bar{\phi}_A \to \phi_B \bar{\phi}_B$, which we found to be $\mathcal{M} = ie^2 \frac{u-t}{s}$.

    We now want to compute the amplitude for the scattering process $\phi_A(p_1) \phi_B(p_2) \to \phi_A(p_3) \phi_B(p_4)$. We can define the crossed momenta $k_i$ for this new process in terms of the original annihilation momenta:
    \[
        k_1 = p_1, \quad k_2 = -p_3, \quad k_3 = p_4, \quad k_4 = -p_2
    \]
    Substituting these crossed momenta into the Mandelstam variables of the original process:
    \begin{align*}
        s(k_i) & = (k_1 + k_2)^2 = (p_1 - p_3)^2 = t \\
        t(k_i) & = (k_3 - k_1)^2 = (p_4 - p_1)^2 = u \\
        u(k_i) & = (k_1 - k_4)^2 = (p_1 + p_2)^2 = s
    \end{align*}
    Therefore, the crossing transformation elegantly maps the Mandelstam variables as $s \to t$, $t \to u$, and $u \to s$. Applying this mapping to the original amplitude gives us the new amplitude immediately:
    \[
        i\mathcal{M}(\phi_A \phi_B \to \phi_A \phi_B) = \left. i\mathcal{M}(\phi_A \bar{\phi}_A \to \phi_B \bar{\phi}_B) \right|_{\substack{s \to t \\ t \to u \\ u \to s}} = ie^2 \frac{s - u}{t}
    \]
    This is the exact same result one would obtain by manually computing the corresponding $t$-channel Feynman diagram for the scattering process.
    \TODO{add diagram: t-channel Feynman diagram for phiA phiB scattering}
\end{example}

As a theoretical extreme, one could use crossing symmetry to compute a completely unphysical amplitude where all particles are in the outgoing state, e.g., $0 \to \phi_A \bar{\phi}_A \phi_B \bar{\phi}_B$ (vacuum decaying into particles). While this amplitude describes a physically impossible process (due to energy conservation), valid physical amplitudes can be effortlessly extracted from it by crossing two of the final state particles back into the initial state.
